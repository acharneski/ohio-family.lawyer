Grandparent and relative rights in Ohio depend heavily on the circumstances: whether the parents were married, whether a parent has died, whether a case is already open, and which state has jurisdiction. This is general information, not advice for your case. Confirm with an attorney.
{% endcallout %}

## The short version

Ohio lets grandparents and other relatives ask a court for companionship or visitation, but only in certain situations, and the court must give significant weight to a fit parent's wishes. You cannot simply file because you were close to the child. You need a legal doorway, and then you need to show that contact is in the child's best interest.

## The legal doorways

In general terms, Ohio law gives relatives a way into court in three situations:

- **During or after a divorce, dissolution, legal separation, annulment, or child support case** involving the child (ORC § 3109.051). A grandparent or other relative can file a motion in that case.
- **When a parent of the child has died** (ORC § 3109.11). That parent's relatives can ask for companionship or visitation.
- **When the child was born to an unmarried mother** (ORC § 3109.12). Relatives of either parent may be able to file, subject to conditions about paternity.

If none of these applies, for example if the parents are still married and no case is open, a grandparent usually has no standing to ask a court for visitation at all, however close the relationship was.

## The "special weight" rule

In *Troxel v. Granville* (2000), the U.S. Supreme Court held that a fit parent's decision about who sees their child is entitled to special weight. Ohio's statutes have been upheld under that standard. In practice, this means:

- You will not win by showing you are a good grandparent. You have to show why contact serves the child, despite the parent's objection.
- Evidence of an existing, substantial relationship, and of what the child loses without it, matters more than evidence about the parent's flaws.
- Courts are wary of relatives who appear to be relitigating the parents' custody dispute.

## If the child moved from another state

Which state's court can hear a visitation request is governed by the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act, which Ohio adopted in ORC Chapter 3127. Key points:

- The child's **home state**, generally where the child lived for the six months before filing, usually has jurisdiction.
- If a court in another state already issued a custody order, that state may keep **continuing jurisdiction** while a parent or the child still lives there.
- So after a move, there may be a window where neither the old state nor the new one is the right place to file, or where the old state is still the right place even though the child is gone.

Talk to an attorney in both states before filing anything.

## Keep a factual record

Your strongest evidence is the relationship itself. Start now, while memories are fresh.

- **Caregiving history:** the years, roughly how many hours a week, and what you did (school drop-off, meals, appointments, overnights, activities).
- **Documents:** school pickup authorizations, emergency-contact forms, appointment records, receipts, photos with dates.
- **Attempts at contact since it stopped:** dates of calls, texts, and letters. Keep returned mail unopened, with the envelope.
- **What you saw versus what you were told.** Write them separately. Courts give much more weight to first-hand observation.

## If you write a declaration

Grandparents are often asked to write a declaration in a parent's case. A declaration that helps the court:

- Sticks to things you personally saw and heard, with approximate dates.
- Describes the child's relationship with you and with each parent, concretely.
- Avoids diagnosing anyone. "She seemed frightened and stayed in the bedroom" is evidence. "She has a psychological problem" is an opinion a court will likely disregard.
- Avoids guessing at motives. Let the facts carry the inference.
- Says plainly where your view has changed over time, and why. Courts notice when a witness's story shifts, and an honest explanation is better than none.
- Is reviewed by the attorney filing it before you sign it under penalty of perjury.

## What not to do

- **Don't try to force contact.** Repeated unannounced visits, contacting children through their school, or sending gifts after a parent has refused them can be framed as harassment and used against you.
- **Don't post about the family online,** and never name the children. Anything you write publicly can become an exhibit.
- **Don't speak badly about either parent to the children,** even if you believe it's deserved. Courts weigh whether a relative will support the child's relationships.
- **Don't wait.** Children age, jurisdiction windows close, and the record of your relationship gets older every month.

## First steps

1. Find out whether a case involving the child is open, in which state, and in which court.
2. Gather the record above.
3. Contact [legal aid](/resources/#directory) or a family-law attorney. Eligibility rules for free help vary.
