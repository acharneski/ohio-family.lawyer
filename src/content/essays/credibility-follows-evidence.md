**Thesis.** Any rule that assigns truth by the identity of the speaker rather than by the evidence is incompatible with due process, and in the end it harms the people it was meant to protect. Credibility should follow evidence, for every claim and every speaker.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that most abuse allegations are false, that women lie more than men, or that reports of abuse should be met with suspicion. It does not argue for weakening emergency protection orders. It argues against one specific mechanism: automatic credibility based on who is speaking.
{% endcallout %}

## I. A Correction That Overshot

"Believe women" began as a corrective, and a necessary one. For generations, people who reported sexual assault and domestic violence were routinely disbelieved, dismissed, or blamed. Police failed to investigate. Institutions protected powerful men. Victims were shamed into silence. The slogan said, in effect: *take these claims seriously; do not reflexively dismiss them.*

In that form it was defensible, and much of the work it did was good. The problem is what happened next. Through repetition and moral momentum, "take claims seriously" hardened in some settings into "always believe": no exceptions, no room for evidence, context, or counter-narrative.

This is a familiar pattern. A justified push against one failure swings far enough to create a new one. An appeal for fairness becomes a demand for automatic deference, and automatic deference is not justice. It is the abdication of it.

## II. Two Commitments That Cannot Coexist

The same people who use the slogan usually hold, as a foundational principle, that sex should never determine how a person is treated: equality before the law, equal dignity, equal consideration.

Those two commitments conflict:

- **A credibility rule tied to identity** elevates a claim because of who makes it, not because of its evidentiary weight.
- **Equal treatment** requires that identity be irrelevant to how a person is judged.

You cannot hold both that sex must never determine how someone is treated and that sex must determine how someone is *believed*. Belief is not a courtesy. It is the gateway to every later consequence: investigation, reputation, custody, employment, and sometimes incarceration.

There is a more precise way to see why the conflict is structural. Testimony works as a signal. Honest and fabricated accounts carry different risks once a claim can be tested against evidence, and that difference is what lets an institution separate them over time. A rule that grants automatic credibility to one class of speaker removes the difference. Truthful and strategic accusers get the same result, because both are believed regardless of what they say. Economists call this moving from a *separating* equilibrium, where honesty is rewarded and fabrication is filtered out, to a *pooling* equilibrium, where every claim is treated the same. That collapse is not a side effect of the rule. It is what the rule does.

## III. Not Hate Speech, Still a Harm

Defenders of the slogan often point out that it is not hate speech: it does not dehumanize or incite violence against anyone. That is true, and it misses the point.

Hate-speech categories were built to catch explicit hostility. They were never designed to catch *structural* harm: rules that do not attack a group directly but quietly redistribute credibility, presumption, and institutional trust. "Always believe" does not say the accused are evil. It says, functionally, that their denials weigh less than the accusation. That is not incitement. It is a thumb on the scale, below the threshold of any statute, with consequences in courtrooms and custody hearings.

## IV. The Collision With Due Process

Our legal system rests on a few hard-won principles: the presumption of innocence, the burden of proof on the party making the claim, the right to confront evidence, and findings before consequences. They exist because human judgment is fallible, and because unchecked accusation, however sincerely believed, has been a tool of oppression as often as a tool of justice.

"Always believe," taken literally, inverts each of them: presumed guilt, a reversed burden, and deference in place of scrutiny. Both cannot operate at once. Where deference wins, accusation begins to function as a verdict, and a defense begins to function as evidence of guilt.

## V. History Already Ran This Experiment

The danger compounds where credibility rules meet racial bias. American history has many examples of what happens when an accusation from one group is treated as inherently more credible than a denial from another: Emmett Till, the Scottsboro Boys, the Central Park Five. In each, automatic credibility ran toward the accuser and against Black defendants, with catastrophic results.

Those cases are not offered to equate anyone with the people who wronged those defendants. They are offered because they show what automatic credibility does, whoever holds it. A rule that tells institutions to "always believe" an accuser does not erase that history. It reactivates the mechanism.

## VI. In Family Court

The damage is most concrete in family courts, where an allegation can shape a child's living arrangements for years.

Family courts already face strong pressure toward caution. A judge who restricts contact on an allegation that turns out false bears little visible cost. A judge who declines to restrict contact on an allegation that turns out true may face public disaster. Add an automatic-belief norm to that asymmetry and the result is predictable. Allegations are treated as established well before any hearing. Contact is curtailed on precautionary logic rather than findings. Some parties learn that an allegation is leverage.

None of this requires believing that most allegations are false. It only requires recognizing that any adversarial process that grants automatic credibility to one side will be exploited by *some* of the people who pass through it. The reason is not who they are but what the system rewards. That is a claim about incentives, and it applies to any group handed the same tool.

## VII. Who Is Harmed

The obvious victims are people wrongly accused, and their children, who may lose a parent for reasons that were never tested.

The less obvious victims are genuine survivors. When a system stops distinguishing corroborated claims from bare assertions, every claim loses value. Juries, judges, and the public learn to discount allegations as a class. Weaponizable systems do not only harm the falsely accused. They erode trust in the claims they were built to protect.

## VIII. Who Benefits

If neither accusers nor accused benefit, who does? Mostly institutions and intermediaries.

For a university, employer, or agency, "we believed the accuser" works as a liability shield. It is individually rational for each institution to adopt it, because the institution does not bear the downstream cost of a wrong finding. The accused bears it, and eventually so do genuine victims, as the system's outputs become less reliable. Lawyers benefit from more motions and hearings. Media benefit from instant villains and instant victims. Advocacy on every side benefits from simple, emotional narratives.

This is not malice. It is misaligned incentives: each institution optimizes for its own exposure rather than accurate adjudication. No single institution has much reason to stop, because the one that adopts a slower, evidence-proportional standard alone takes the reputational risk of appearing to "side with the accused."

## IX. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**False allegations are rare, and disbelief is still the bigger problem.** Most survivors never report, and many who do are not believed.

**"Believe women" never meant "convict on accusation."** It meant "start from belief and investigate," and critics attack a straw man.

**Courts cannot wait for proof when a child may be in danger.** Some restrictions must come before the facts are known.
{% endcallout %}

**On rarity.** We agree that under-reporting and disbelief remain serious, and nothing here argues for returning to them. But a rule should be judged by what it does to *every* case it touches, including the minority it handles badly. A standard that is right most of the time and unappealable the rest of the time is not a good standard.

**On meaning.** Where the slogan means "take every claim seriously and investigate," we have no quarrel with it. That is exactly our position. The problem is the literal reading, which some institutions have adopted as a decision rule. We are arguing against the rule, not the people who meant something better.

**On emergencies.** Correct, and that is why emergency protection must stay fast. The answer is not slower protection. It is a quick hearing after the emergency, and findings before a restriction continues.

{% callout "protections" %}
Ex parte and emergency civil protection orders under ORC § 3113.31 must remain fast and low-barrier. Nothing in this essay argues for raising the bar to emergency protection. Our proposal ([priority 9](/reform/agenda/evidence-standards-allegations/)) shortens the time to a full hearing and requires findings before contact is restricted beyond the emergency period.
{% endcallout %}

## X. What Should Replace It

The alternative to "always believe" is not "never believe." That would repeat the same mistake in reverse. The alternative is the only standard that has reliably produced justice: **believe claims in proportion to the evidence that supports them.**

That means:

- taking every allegation seriously enough to investigate it promptly and thoroughly;
- preserving due process even when it is emotionally uncomfortable, because that is exactly when it matters;
- weighing corroboration, consistency, motive, and context over the identity of the speaker;
- imposing consequences for *knowingly* false allegations, which protects genuine reporters by protecting the value of a report.

This standard is easy to state and hard to sustain, because no single institution profits from adopting it alone. That is why it has to be written into rules that bind every court in the state, rather than left to each judge's tolerance for risk. In Ohio, that means expedited hearings on emergency restrictions, written findings before restrictions continue, and consistent standards across all 88 counties.

## Conclusion

"Always believe" did not begin as an attack on anyone, and calling it hatred obscures more than it reveals. But as a literal rule, it asks a culture that prizes equality to adopt a credibility hierarchy because it points in a favored direction. It cannot demand that identity be irrelevant to how people are treated and determinative of how they are believed. It cannot protect victims while rewarding the dynamics that cheapen every victim's claim.

The way forward is not a louder slogan pointed the other way. It is the unglamorous work of building systems that take every claim seriously, test every claim fairly, and give credibility to evidence rather than identity. That is not a betrayal of victims. It is the only foundation on which justice for victims, and for everyone else, can be built.
