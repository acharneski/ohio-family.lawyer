**Thesis.** A parenting order is a set of obligations on two people. When one side's obligations are monitored by paid professionals who report in real time, and the other side's are monitored by no one, the order is not neutral, whatever it says on paper. And when contact is the only part of the order that depends on a parent's ability to pay, money, not risk, decides when a child loses a parent.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that supervised parenting time is never justified, that supervisors act in bad faith, or that any particular parent should not have been supervised. Supervision protects children in real danger. The claim is about structure: how supervised orders are funded, monitored, reviewed, and ended.
{% endcallout %}

## I. The Supervised Hour

Picture the arrangement as it often works. A parent drives to a center or meets a paid supervisor. He pays by the hour. He sees his children in a room, or a park, or a fast-food restaurant, while someone takes notes. The notes go to a coordinator, a guardian ad litem, or the court. Then he goes home and waits a week to do it again.

Parents who have lived this often describe the visits themselves as the hardest part of the case, harder than the hearings and harder than the missed birthdays. That should not be surprising. A hearing happens once. The supervised hour happens every week. It's a recurring reminder, paid for by the person it reminds, that the system has classified him as a risk to his own children. Often that classification rests on findings that were never made, under a schedule the other side is not required to honor.

None of this means the supervisors are the problem. Many are careful and humane. The problem is everything around the hour.

## II. Who Is Monitored, and Who Is Not

Read a typical phased parenting plan with one question in mind: *whose compliance does anyone actually check?*

On one side are a paid visit supervisor, a parenting coordinator, a reunification counselor, and sometimes remote substance testing that reports results to the coordinator or the court. Treatment records may be reviewed. Every lapse is recorded by someone whose job is to record it.

On the other side are obligations such as "encourage the relationship," "don't interfere with calls," and "share the children's phone numbers." Nobody is paid to watch those. If they are ignored, the remedy is a motion: a lawyer, a filing fee, a hearing date months away, all paid for by the parent who was wronged.

An order with that shape produces a predictable record. One parent generates a thick file of monitored behavior, where any slip is documented. The other generates almost nothing, because nothing was measured. When the case comes back to court, the thick file looks like evidence of risk, and the empty one looks like evidence of compliance. Neither impression is about the parents. Both are about the instrumentation.

## III. Enforcement Is Billable. Noncompliance Is Free.

Every step toward more contact costs money: the supervisor, the coordinator, the counselor, the testing, the attorney, the motions. Not following the schedule costs nothing unless the other parent pays to prove it.

Compare child support. Support is withheld from wages automatically, tracked by a county agency, and backed by license suspension and contempt. The agency doesn't need the receiving parent to hire a lawyer. It is funded, in part, by federal incentive payments tied to collections. See [priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/).
<!-- CITE: 42 U.S.C. § 658a incentive structure; Ohio CSEA enforcement tools. -->

So the money keeps flowing whether or not the parent is seeing his children. Parenting time is the one part of the order that depends on the other parent's cooperation, and the one part that stops when he can't pay.

## IV. The Off Switch Is Money

Here is the failure mode in its simplest form. A supervised parent loses his income. Months pass before he finds work. During that time he cannot pay the supervisor. Contact stops.

No court found that the children were less safe that year. No hearing was held. The order didn't change. The parent's bank account did. If he wants contact back, he has to restart a process he already paid for, with the same fees, the same monitoring, and the same uncertainty about whether the other side will comply this time.

Many parents in that position make a rational calculation and decline to pay for another round. That's not because they stopped caring. Their experience told them the system engages only as far as someone is paying. From the outside, that looks like a parent who "gave up." From the inside, it's an accurate reading of the incentives.

## V. Time Is Not Neutral

Supervised orders often have no end date and no written criteria for stepping down. That would be bad enough in any setting. In a custody case it is worse, because the children keep aging.

A child who was nine when supervision started may be sixteen when anyone gets around to reviewing it. At eighteen, the order no longer governs contact at all. A process that moves at the speed of motions and billing can outlast a childhood without ever reaching a decision about whether supervision was needed. Delay isn't a neutral holding pattern. It's a decision, made by default.

## VI. The Records Bind

Supervised plans often tie progress toward unsupervised time to treatment: counseling, assessments, testing, and the records that come out of them. That creates a second trap.

A parent who seeks therapy for the stress of the case creates records that can be reviewed by appointees and used in the case. A parent who doesn't is described as resistant or in denial. There is no choice that can't be counted against him. The predictable result is that the parents under the most strain are the ones with the strongest reason to avoid care. [Priority 14](/reform/agenda/treatment-is-not-testimony/) addresses this directly. See also [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/).

## VII. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**Supervision exists because some parents are dangerous.** Removing it, or making it cheaper to leave, puts children at risk.

**Someone has to pay for supervision,** and the parent whose conduct led to it is the obvious candidate.

**Monitoring the other parent's "encouragement" is impossible.** You can't instrument an attitude.
{% endcallout %}

**On danger.** Agreed, and nothing here removes supervision where a risk has been found. The proposal is that the risk be stated in writing, reviewed on a date certain, and tied to criteria a parent can actually meet. A child in real danger stays protected under that rule. A parent who was never shown to be a danger gets a path out.

**On cost.** If a court found a specific risk, charging the parent whose conduct created it is defensible. But many supervised orders are entered as precautions, on unadjudicated allegations, early in a case. In those cases, "the parent who caused it" assumes the conclusion. And whoever pays, a child's contact with a parent shouldn't end because a bill went unpaid.

**On instrumentation.** Partly true. But much of the other side's obligation is concrete and checkable. Did the exchange happen? Was the phone number provided? Were the calls answered? A coordinator who already receives real-time reports on one parent can log those facts about the other. Courts could also count missed parenting time as routinely as they count missed support payments.

{% callout "protections" %}
Supervision remains available wherever a court finds a real risk to a child, and emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31 stays fast and low-barrier. These proposals change how supervision is reviewed, funded, and ended. They do not lower the bar for protecting a child.
{% endcallout %}

## VIII. What Would Fix It

- **Every supervised order states its reason, a review date, and step-down criteria.** That means no open-ended supervision and no moving targets. See [priority 13](/reform/agenda/supervision-with-an-exit/).
- **Findings are required to continue supervision past review.** The burden belongs on the party seeking restriction, not on the parent trying to end it.
- **Contact never ends only for inability to pay.** Use sliding-scale fees or court-funded centers, so money doesn't stand in for risk.
- **Both parents' compliance is tracked equally.** If one side's lapses are logged in real time, so are the other's.
- **Treatment records are used only for what they're relevant to.** Draw no adverse inference from seeking care or from declining it. See [priority 14](/reform/agenda/treatment-is-not-testimony/).
- **The data is counted.** That includes how many supervised orders are entered each year, how long they last, how many end by court order, and how many end because the payments stopped. See [priority 12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/).

## Conclusion

An order that monitors one parent and trusts the other isn't neutral. It's a measuring device pointed in one direction. A contact schedule that ends when the money does is not a judgment about a child's safety. It's a billing event. And supervision with no review date isn't caution. It's a decision deferred until the child ages out and nobody has to make it.

Protecting children from real danger and keeping them connected to parents who aren't dangerous are not competing goals. A system that can't tell the two cases apart has to pay for that failure somewhere. Right now, the cost lands on the parent least able to bear it and on the children who lose him.
