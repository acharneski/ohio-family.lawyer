**Thesis.** When a court orders two parents to communicate only through a paid app, it is not just choosing a messaging tool. It is choosing what the record of the case will contain. A record that captures every word, and very little of what a parent does or fails to do, tends to favor whichever parent writes less and acts offline. In our view, the software is not the defect. The order is.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that OurFamilyWizard or any other company acts in bad faith, misrepresents its product, mishandles data, or harms anyone by design. We have no reason to think any of these products fails to do what its maker says it does. Many families, including families escaping abuse, may find such tools genuinely useful.

This site is not affiliated with or endorsed by any provider. Product names are used only to identify the kind of product being discussed. Descriptions of how these apps work are general and reflect our understanding of the category. They have not been independently verified, and they may not describe any particular product, version, or plan. Statements about structure, incentives, and what courts should do are the author's opinion. The claim is about structure: what happens when courts make a private product the official channel of a family's communication, and then read its output as evidence.
{% endcallout %}

## I. The Order

The language is short, and orders like it are not unusual. *The parties shall communicate exclusively through [a named co-parenting application], except in an emergency.* Some orders name a specific product, and OurFamilyWizard is one widely recognized example. Others describe the required function, or leave the choice to the parents. We have not surveyed Ohio orders or local rules. We make no claim about how often any product is named or about the practice of any particular court or county.

On its own terms, the order makes sense. Parents who cannot speak civilly by text or phone get a single, structured channel. Nobody can claim a message was never sent. Nobody can quietly delete what they said at midnight. The court gets a clean record if it ever needs one.

Now look at who is involved in the decision. The judge who orders the app does not pay for the parents' subscriptions. Neither does the guardian ad litem who recommends it. Some providers in this category offer accounts for attorneys, mediators, or GALs on different terms than parent accounts, sometimes at reduced or no cost. We have not reviewed any particular provider's current terms. The parents generally pay for their own access. They typically did not choose the product, and they may not be able to switch without going back to court.

Some providers also offer fee assistance for parents who qualify, and that is to their credit. But assistance a parent must apply for still leaves a cost, and a step, between that parent and the channel the court requires.

When this happens, the people who choose the product don't pay for it, and the people who pay for it don't choose it. That is not a description of any company's conduct. It is a description of a market in which the purchasing decision is made by a court order.

## II. A Record Built for Words

Products in this category are commonly presented as record-keeping systems as much as messaging systems. Typical features include:

- messages that cannot be edited or deleted after sending;
- time stamps, sometimes including when a message was first viewed;
- shared calendars and schedule-change requests;
- expense tracking;
- exportable reports intended for use in court.

Not every product or plan includes all of these, and features change over time. We are describing the category, not any particular product.

That combination is a remarkable instrument for capturing one kind of evidence: what each parent wrote, and when.

It is a much weaker instrument for capturing what each parent did. The app can show that a father asked for his weekend. It can show the mother's reply, or her silence. It cannot show the driveway on Friday at six, who was standing in it, and whether the children came out. Some apps let a parent log a location at an exchange, which may help document that one parent showed up. But a record of where one person was standing is still not a record of whether the other person produced the children.

So the record a court gets from the app is detailed in one dimension and thin in the other. Words are logged thoroughly. Conduct is logged only when someone writes about it.

## III. Every Word an Exhibit

Permanence changes how people write. A parent who knows that every message may be printed, highlighted, and handed to a magistrate stops writing to the other parent and starts writing to the judge. The app was supposed to make communication safer. In high-conflict cases, it can make communication theatrical.

That shift does not land evenly. Consider two parents.

- **The other writes rarely.** She replies briefly and makes decisions offline. She produces almost nothing to quote.

When the file reaches court, the thick side looks like the conflict. The thin side looks like calm. Neither impression is necessarily about the parents. It is about what the instrument measures. This is the same asymmetry described in [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/), with a subscription fee instead of an hourly one: one parent's conduct generates evidence, and the other's generates none.

Where an app shows when a message was viewed, the asymmetry sharpens. A timestamp showing a message was viewed and not answered is useful when the question is notice. But silence is not evidence of anything in particular, while a reply is evidence of whatever it says. The parent with the most to say about the case is the one with the most to lose by saying it.

## IV. Tone Is Measurable. Truth Is Not.

Some co-parenting apps include an optional tone-checking feature that flags potentially charged language in a draft message before it is sent. As such features are generally described, they are advisory: a parent can revise the message or send it anyway. We have not examined how any particular feature works, whether it is on by default, or whether its prompts appear in any exported record.

As a personal tool, that is a kind idea. It is in the spirit of the advice in [The Second Arrow](/essays/the-second-arrow/): hold the message up to the mirror before it leaves you. We have no objection to software that helps a parent breathe before hitting send.

The structural concern is narrower. Tone is the one thing in a co-parenting dispute that is easy to measure automatically. Truth is not.

Take a sentence like *you have kept the children from me for three weekends in a row*. It may be accurate, documented, and legally significant. It is also, unavoidably, charged. We have not tested how any tone filter scores that sentence, and we make no claim about it. The point is what tone-focused tools can encourage everyone in the process to assume, including the professionals reading the output: that the quality of a message is how it sounds, not whether it is true. Credibility should follow evidence, not delivery; see [Credibility Follows Evidence](/essays/credibility-follows-evidence/).

In our view, family courts too often treat objection as temperament. Ohio's best-interest factors ask which parent is more likely to honor and facilitate court-approved parenting time. In practice, a parent who protests forcefully can end up described as "high conflict" ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)). Combine an emphasis on tone with a court that reads tone as evidence, and you complete the loop described in [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/). Protest becomes a data point against the protester, now with a time stamp.

## V. What the Record Can't See

Return to the asymmetry in [Two Sets of Books](/essays/two-sets-of-books/). Unpaid support is detected by a state agency and arrives at a custody hearing as an official printout. Denied parenting time is detected by no one, and arrives, if at all, as a parent's word.

A court-ordered app could, in principle, help close that gap. As we understand the category, these apps generally do not, because they were never asked to. They are not typically connected to the terms of the court order or to any enforcement process. The app records a request and a refusal, or a request and no answer, and then nothing happens. No agency is notified. No missed-time balance accrues. The parent who lost the weekend still has to hire a lawyer, file a motion, and wait months. Now he gets to attach a well-formatted PDF.

That is not a failure by any vendor. These products are offered as communication and record-keeping tools. The failure, in our view, is the court's failure to ask what the record is for. A court that orders a system making every word permanent, while leaving every missed exchange to private litigation, has instrumented the cheap evidence. It has left the expensive evidence to the parent least able to pay for it.

## VI. The Customer Is the Court

In any market, products tend to be shaped by whoever makes the purchasing decision. When a court order requires use of a product, that decision is effectively made by courts and the professionals who advise them, not by the parents who use it.

Nothing about that implies impropriety by anyone. Making a product known to judges, attorneys, and GALs, including through professional access, is an ordinary way to make sure the people who order it understand how it works.

But structure shapes design, whatever anyone's intentions. As far as we can tell, courts ordering these apps have mainly wanted a clean, exportable, uneditable record of what the parents said, and that is what the category is known for. We are not aware of an app built to alert the court every time a scheduled exchange fails to happen, and to keep alerting it until someone acts. As far as we know, courts have not asked for one either. If courts want that, they will have to specify it.

This is a version of the pattern in [The Church of CYA](/essays/the-church-of-cya/), in software form. The court gets a defensible file. The family gets a subscription cost and a permanent transcript of its worst years. Whether any of this reduces conflict, or improves outcomes for children, is something Ohio courts have not, to our knowledge, measured. Providers may publish their own user surveys. We have not evaluated them, and they are not a substitute for independent measurement.

## VII. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**These apps reduce conflict.** Parents who were fighting by text at 2 a.m. now have structure, a cooling-off prompt, and a reason to be civil.

**They protect victims of abuse.** A survivor can communicate without a phone number, without direct contact, and with a permanent record of every threat.

**The app is neutral.** It records both parents identically. If one parent's messages look bad, the problem is the messages.

**Just don't write anything you wouldn't want a judge to read.**
{% endcallout %}

**On conflict.** Possibly, for many families, and we have no evidence otherwise. That is exactly why it should be measured rather than assumed. A court that orders a product to reduce conflict should want to know whether it did.

**On abuse.** This is the strongest point, and we agree with it fully. For a parent being threatened or harassed, a permanent, uneditable channel with no direct contact can be a genuine protection. Nothing here argues against making that tool available. The argument is against treating the same tool as neutral in cases where no abuse has been found, and against reading its output as though words were the whole case.

**On neutrality.** The software may apply the same rules to both parents. The record it produces does not fall on them equally. A measuring device that only measures one kind of behavior will tend to favor whoever engages in the other kind. Identical treatment of unequal positions is not neutrality.

**On writing carefully.** Good advice for any parent, and we give it ourselves. But a system whose evidence depends on which parent is better at writing for a judge is measuring literacy, composure, and legal coaching. It is not measuring parenting.

{% callout "protections" %}
Courts should keep every tool that protects a parent or child from threats, harassment, or abuse. That includes no-contact communication channels and emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31. Nothing in this essay argues against a parent choosing any app, or against a court ordering one where a need has been shown. The proposals change who pays, what the record captures, and how it is read.
{% endcallout %}

## VIII. What Would Fix It

- **Whoever orders it pays for it, or offers a free option.** If a court requires a specific paid product, the cost should be waived by default for parents below a stated income. Alternatively, the court should accept any app or channel that meets defined record-keeping standards, including free ones. In our view, a parent should not have to pay for the only channel through which a court allows them to discuss their child.
- **Order the function, not the brand.** Orders should specify what the channel must do, such as permanence, time stamps, and export, rather than naming one product, unless a party shows a reason.
- **Record conduct, not just words.** Any court-ordered system should let a parent log a missed or refused exchange against the schedule in the order. Courts should track those records as routinely as they track missed support ([priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/)).
- **Read the record whole.** Excerpts from app records should not be used to characterize a parent as "high conflict" without the full context and independent findings ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)). Tone is not a finding.
- **Disclose any arrangement.** A professional who recommends a specific product may have special access to it or another arrangement with its provider. Disclosing that on the record would match the transparency expected of other appointment-related arrangements ([priority 5](/reform/agenda/gal-evaluator-reform/)). We do not suggest that any such arrangement influences any recommendation. Disclosure simply removes the question.
- **Count it.** Courts should report how often they order co-parenting apps, at whose expense, and how those cases turn out ([priorities 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/) and [12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/)).

## Conclusion

A court-ordered co-parenting app can do exactly what it is meant to do. It can keep a durable record of what two parents write to each other.

That is not a record of the case. It is a record of the part of the case that is easiest to print. What gets captured, and how it is read, is up to the court. The court chose the instrument, the court reads from it, and the court can decide to ask for more.
