**Thesis.** A tool does not know whose hand it is in. Interim orders without hearings, recommendations without accountability, supervision without an exit, and protest treated as pathology feel reasonable to the people who use them because they are always used on someone else. This story turns the tools around once, without adding any cruelty of its own, to see whether they still look reasonable from the other side of the table.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This is fiction. Gerald Pike is not based on any real attorney, guardian ad litem, visitation provider, or judge, and no real county, court, or business is depicted. The story does not claim that people in these roles are villains, that they deserve punishment, or that anyone's death is a lesson. Pike is written as a decent man by his own lights, which is the point. The claim is structural: the tools in this story are real, and they work the same way no matter who they are pointed at.
{% endcallout %}

## Prologue

*Consider Gerald Ambrose Pike, fifty-eight years old, thirty-one of them spent in the domestic relations building of a mid-sized Ohio county. You know the type even if you have never met him. As a lawyer he could get an emergency order signed before lunch. As a guardian ad litem he wrote reports that judges adopted nearly word for word. On the side he co-owned Safe Harbor Family Visitation Services, two rooms and a parking lot, forty dollars an hour, where fathers met their children under fluorescent light while a monitor took notes.*

*Tonight Gerald Pike has been elected judge. He is standing at a podium in a hotel ballroom, thanking his wife, his daughter, and "every family I've had the privilege to serve." Halfway through the word "privilege," the left side of his face stops cooperating.*

*Gerald Pike is about to take the bench. Not the one he campaigned for. A different one, in a building with very familiar furniture, in a jurisdiction we might call the Judgment Zone.*

## I. Intake

He woke on a bench in a hallway.

He knew the hallway before he knew he was awake. Beige walls. A drinking fountain that hummed. A row of doors with paper signs taped at eye level: *Magistrate*, *Mediation*, *Assignment*. The air smelled like toner and floor wax. At the end of the hall was a clerk's window with a bell.

He rang it. A woman looked up. She had the face of every clerk he had ever thanked by first name and never once asked about.

"Case number?"

"I don't have a case. I think there's been a mistake. I was in the middle of a speech."

She typed. "Pike, Gerald A. Here you are." She slid a sheet of paper under the glass. "Interim order. You'll want to read paragraph three."

He read it the way he had read thousands of them, fast, looking for the operative line.

*Pending further hearing, all contact between Respondent and his family shall be supervised. Respondent shall bear the cost of supervision.*

"Supervised by whom? On what basis? There's no finding here."

"A concern has been raised."

"By whom?"

"That's confidential at this stage."

He laughed, because he knew this part. This part he could fix. "Fine. I'd like to file a motion for an expedited hearing."

"Filing fee is two hours."

"Two hours of what?"

She looked at him with a patience he recognized. It was the patience he used on clients who had not yet understood how things worked.

"Of time. Everything here is billed in hours. You arrived with a balance." She turned her screen so he could see it. The number was very large.

"Where did that come from?"

"Every hour you billed a family, you've been credited. Attorney time, guardian time, supervision time. You were very productive." She tapped the screen. "Your visits come out of the same account. So does everything else. Spend it how you like."

He paid the two hours. The receipt printed with a hearing date that said only *Next Available*.

## II. The Guardian

The guardian ad litem met him in a small room with a round table and a box of tissues placed exactly where he used to place them.

Her name was Ms. Ostrander. She was pleasant, organized, and wrote in a spiral notebook. She asked about his marriage, his work hours, his relationship with his daughter.

"Claire is fourteen," he said. "We're close. I worked a lot, I'll grant that. But I was there for the important things."

"Which important things?"

He named a recital, a hospital visit, a birthday dinner he'd left early to take a call. He heard himself say the last one and wished he hadn't.

The interview lasted forty minutes. The invoice, delivered the next morning, billed 2.4 hours. It included 0.2 for "review of correspondence" and 0.3 for "case strategy." He had used both line items for years.

Her report arrived a week later. He read it on the bench in the hallway.

*Mr. Pike presented as articulate and knowledgeable about court procedure. However, he appeared more focused on the process than on his daughter's needs. He was unable to identify specific examples of his involvement in Claire's daily life without prompting.*

He knew that paragraph. He had written that paragraph. Not a version of it. That paragraph, with the semicolon he always put in the second sentence and then took out before filing.

## III. The Supervised Hour

The visitation room had two chairs, a table bolted to the floor, and a wide pane of glass where a window should be. On the other side of the glass was his own kitchen.

Claire sat at the table with a bowl of cereal she was not eating. It was a school morning. A sympathy card was still on the counter. His wife was on the phone in the next room, using the voice she used with insurance companies.

The monitor sat in the corner with a clipboard. The form on the clipboard had a faded logo at the top, a lighthouse inside a circle. *Safe Harbor Family Visitation Services. Form SH-4: Observation Notes.* He had designed the form. He had chosen the lighthouse.

"I'm allowed to talk to her?" he asked.

"She can't hear you. You're allowed to be present. One hour, weekly, at your expense."

He put his hand on the glass. He said Claire's name. He said he was sorry about the birthday dinner. He said it again, louder, as if volume were the problem.

At the end of the hour the monitor read back her notes. She read them in a neutral voice, the voice he had trained his monitors to use.

*Father became tearful within the first ten minutes. Father attempted physical contact through the barrier after being redirected. Father raised adult topics with the child ("I'm sorry about the dinner"). Recommend continued supervision.*

"I'm her father," he said. "I'm dead. What exactly is the risk?"

The monitor wrote that down too.

He asked, on the way out, who was supervising the other side. Who was writing down whether his wife let Claire talk about him, whether anyone kept the photographs out, whether the family was facilitating the relationship.

"Nobody," said the monitor. "That's not the side with the concern."

## IV. Status Conference

At the status conference the magistrate had his file open. It was already thick. His side of it was thick, anyway. The other side was a single page labeled *Concern*.

He made the argument he would have made for any good client. No findings. No hearing. An interim order now in its fourth month. Costs that kept him from the only contact he was allowed. He cited the rules. He cited due process. He raised his voice once, at the end, because it was true and nobody was listening.

The magistrate thanked him. The order that came out the next day said:

*Respondent continues to focus on perceived procedural defects rather than on the needs of the family. The guardian ad litem reports escalating conflict. Respondent is encouraged to engage constructively with the process.*

So he engaged constructively. At the next conference he sat still, spoke softly, agreed with everything, and asked for nothing.

*Respondent presented with flat affect and appeared disengaged. The court has concerns about Respondent's level of investment.*

He sat in the hallway afterward and did arithmetic. If anger counted against him and calm counted against him, then the variable they were measuring was not his conduct. It was him being in the file.

He had explained this to a father once. A carpenter, big hands, crying in this same hallway. Gerald had told him, kindly, that the system wasn't personal. He had meant it as comfort.

## V. Treatment

The next order required a psychological evaluation and twelve sessions of counseling, both at his expense, both reporting to the guardian.

The counselor was good. That was the worst part. She was warm and unhurried, and the room had a lamp instead of overhead lights, and for the first time since the ballroom he said true things out loud. That he was frightened. That he had spent his daughter's childhood in other people's divorces. That he had once advised a client to stop asking for his son back because the fees would never end, and the client had taken the advice, and Gerald had felt relieved.

Ms. Ostrander's supplemental report quoted session notes in a footnote.

*Respondent acknowledges a history of prioritizing work over family and reports persistent anxiety. Respondent expressed regret regarding prior professional conduct, which this writer finds concerning as to his judgment.*

He did not go to session nine. The report noted that too. *Respondent has disengaged from recommended services.*

## VI. Mediation

The mediator's office had a plant and a water pitcher and a poster about listening.

"I want to be realistic with you," the mediator said, which was the sentence Gerald used to open every settlement talk he ever ran. "Your balance is running down. Supervision, guardian, evaluation, counseling, filings. At this rate you'll be through it well before the hearing."

"When is the hearing?"

She checked. "The next available date is after your daughter's eighteenth birthday."

"After she's eighteen the order doesn't govern anything."

"Then it resolves itself." She said it gently. "Here's what I can offer. Agree to continued supervision with no fixed end date. Withdraw the objections. The guardian's fees stop accruing, and the file closes as a consent order. Or you can wait for the hearing."

He looked at the pitcher, the plant, the poster. He thought about every parent he had sat across from in a room like this, doing the arithmetic he was doing now: not whether the deal was fair, but whether they could afford to refuse it.

"If I say no," he said, "what does that make me?"

"High conflict," she said, not unkindly. "You know that."

He did know that.

## VII. The Bench

He did not sign. He spent most of what was left of his balance on one thing: an evidentiary hearing, now, on whatever the concern was. The clerk said nobody had asked for that before. She seemed to mean it as a compliment.

The courtroom was the one he had run for in his campaign ads. Oak paneling. The state seal. A judge on the bench in a plain black robe, a calm man with reading glasses, turning pages.

"Mr. Pike. You've requested findings."

"I have. I want to know what I'm accused of. I want to know who says so. I want a date when this ends, and I want to know what I have to do to get there."

The judge nodded slowly. "Those are reasonable requests. They are not, however, part of the local rules."

"Whose local rules?"

The judge turned the binder around. The cover page was the county's domestic relations rules, the edition Gerald had helped revise on the bar committee eleven years ago. His initials were in the margin of the supervision section. He had argued, at that committee, against adding review dates. *It ties the court's hands*, he'd said. *Every family is different.*

"Who designed this place?" he asked.

"Nobody designed it," said the judge. "We adopted your rules. They were very thorough. People here are only doing what you did, the way you did it. Nobody is being unkind to you, Mr. Pike. We followed the procedure."

"So this is hell."

"Hell would have a purpose." The judge closed the binder. "This is just the standard schedule."

## VIII. Appointment

His balance ran out on a Tuesday. The clerk explained that when an account reached zero, a resident could earn hours the only way hours were earned here: by taking appointments.

She slid a file under the glass. A new arrival, a woman, forty-one, her own interim order still warm from the printer. *A concern has been raised.* At the bottom, a line: *Guardian ad litem: Pike, Gerald A.*

He took the file to the round table with the tissue box and sat with it a long time. He had a spiral notebook. He had thirty-one years of sentences that came to his pen without his asking. *Appears more focused on the process. Unable to identify specific examples. Recommend continued supervision.*

He wrote the date at the top of the page. Under it he wrote: *No findings have been made.* Under that, in block letters he hadn't used since law school: *REVIEW DATE*, and then a date, thirty days out, and then three things the woman could actually do to get there.

He did not know whether anyone would read it. He did not know whether the rules allowed it. He suspected they did not.

He signed it anyway.

## Epilogue

*Gerald Pike spent three decades building tools for a system he was sure he would never face. He built them well. They were efficient, defensible, and easy to bill, and they worked exactly the same way when the man on the other side of the glass was him.*

*The Judgment Zone is not a punishment. Nobody there hates him. It is only a place where the rules are applied to everyone, including the people who wrote them. In most places, that is called fairness. Somewhere in a quiet hallway, a man has just written one review date into one file. It isn't much. In this jurisdiction, it is very nearly a revolution.*

*Next time you hear someone say "we followed the procedure," ask who the procedure was written for. And ask who it was written by.*

## Afterword: The Tools in the Story

Every device in the Judgment Zone exists in some form outside it. None of them requires a villain. Each one has a fix.

- **An interim restriction on an untested "concern," with a hearing at the next available date.** See [Credibility Follows Evidence](/essays/credibility-follows-evidence/). Fix: a prompt hearing after any emergency order, and findings before a restriction continues ([priority 9](/reform/agenda/evidence-standards-allegations/)).
- **A guardian whose report recycles stock phrases, billed to the person being evaluated, with complaints routed through the appointing court.** See [Two Sets of Books](/essays/two-sets-of-books/), Section VIII. Fix: published rates, rotation, conflict disclosure, and outside complaint review ([priority 5](/reform/agenda/gal-evaluator-reform/)).
- **Supervision at the parent's own expense, with one side monitored and the other not, and no end date.** See [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/). Fix: a stated reason, a review date, and step-down criteria for every supervised order ([priority 13](/reform/agenda/supervision-with-an-exit/)), and equal tracking of both parents' compliance ([priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/)).
- **A settlement offered under a hearing date that arrives after the child turns eighteen.** See [Splitting the Baby](/essays/splitting-the-baby/). Fix: findings before bargaining, and firm hearing timelines.
- **A file that is thick on one side and a single page on the other, and nobody who ever counts how cases like this turn out.** See [The Church of CYA](/essays/the-church-of-cya/). Fix: outcome measurement and public aggregate data ([priorities 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/) and [12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/)).

{% callout "objections" %}
**This is a revenge fantasy against professionals.**

**Most guardians, supervisors, and family lawyers are careful and decent people.**

**Fiction proves nothing.**
{% endcallout %}

**On revenge.** The Zone adds no cruelty of its own. Every hardship Pike faces is one he used, in the same form, at the same price. If the result reads as punishment, that is information about the tools, not about the man. The story also ends with him choosing differently, which a revenge story would not allow.

**On decency.** Agreed, and Pike is written that way. He is kind to the carpenter in the hallway. He believes the system is not personal. The argument of this site is that decent people inside a badly designed system still produce the system's results. Decency is not a substitute for a review date.

**On proof.** Correct. The story is a thought experiment, not evidence. The evidence, and its gaps, are in the essays linked above. What the story offers is a test anyone in the system can run on any rule: would you accept it if you were on the other side of the glass?

{% callout "protections" %}
Nothing here argues against supervision where a court has found a real risk, against emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31, or against confidentiality for families. Those protections stay fast and available. The proposals change how restrictions are reviewed, paid for, and ended when no danger has been found.
{% endcallout %}
- **Distress read as risk, and both anger and calm counted against him.** See [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/) and [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), Section III. Fix: objection treated as information, not "high conflict," without independent findings ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)).
- **Counseling records quoted back in a report, and leaving counseling counted too.** See [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/), Section VI. Fix: treatment records used only for what they are relevant to, with no adverse inference from seeking care or from declining it ([priority 14](/reform/agenda/treatment-is-not-testimony/)).
