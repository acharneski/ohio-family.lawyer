**Thesis.** The judgment of Solomon is remembered as the model of judicial wisdom. Read as institutional design, it is something else. It shows a ruler who ended a dispute between two powerless women in one hearing by threatening to kill a child. He investigated nothing, checked no outcome, and was praised because the people feared him. Modern family law inherited both halves of that story. One is the oracle judge who will see through to the truth at trial. The other is the settlement extracted under the threat of the sword. The two tracks rarely inform each other, and allegations end up bargained over rather than decided.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not attack anyone's faith, claim that 1 Kings is a court transcript, or argue that mediation is bad for families. Most separating parents are better served by agreement than by trial. It does not claim that the Solomon story literally created the modern split between litigation and alternative dispute resolution, which has its own twentieth-century history. It claims that the story is the cultural template both tracks borrow from, and that the template carries a design flaw.
{% endcallout %}

## I. The Case

The facts, as 1 Kings 3:16–28 gives them, are short. Two women share a house. Both have given birth within three days. One infant dies in the night, smothered because his mother "overlaid it" (3:19). Each woman says the living child is hers. There is no witness: "there was no stranger with us in the house" (3:18).

The king says, "Bring me a sword." He orders the living child divided and half given to each woman. One woman begs him to give the child to the other rather than kill it. The other says, "Let it be neither mine nor thine, but divide it." Solomon gives the child to the first woman. The story ends: "And all Israel heard of the judgment which the king had judged; and they feared the king" (3:28).

That is the whole record. It is the most cited custody decision in Western history, and it runs to thirteen verses.

## II. Not Mad. Worse.

It is tempting to call a man who orders a baby cut in half insane. The story rules that out, and the alternative is more troubling. Solomon is not raving. He is calm, and he has a plan. The sword is a tool, chosen in advance, for extracting information the court could not otherwise get.

The usual defense is that he never meant to do it. That may be true, but a bluff only works if it is believed. The true mother cries out because she thinks the king might do exactly what he said. The trick runs entirely on the credibility of the state's willingness to harm the child. A court that can only find the truth by convincing a parent it is prepared to destroy what she loves has not shown wisdom. It has shown leverage.

This site does not diagnose people, including literary kings (see [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/)). The charge here is not madness. It is design.

## III. A Bad King by His Own Book's Standard

No modern critique is needed to call Solomon a bad ruler. His own scripture supplies the checklist.

Deuteronomy 17:16–17 warns that a king must not multiply horses, must not send the people back to Egypt for them, must not multiply wives, and must not greatly multiply silver and gold. 1 Kings records Solomon doing each. He imported horses and chariots from Egypt (10:28–29). He made silver in Jerusalem "as stones" (10:27). He had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (11:3).

He secured his throne by killing. His half-brother Adonijah was executed (2:25). Joab was killed while holding the horns of the altar (2:28–34). Shimei was executed on a pretext (2:46). The chapter closes: "And the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon."

He built with conscripted labor. 1 Kings 5:13–14 describes a levy of thirty thousand men sent to Lebanon in shifts. Chapter 9 insists that Israelites were not made bondmen (9:22), and readers have argued about that tension for centuries. What is not in dispute is how the people remembered it. After his death they asked his son for relief: "Thy father made our yoke grievous" (12:4). The son refused, and the kingdom split in two.
<!-- CITE: 1 Kings 2, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12; Deuteronomy 17:14–20. Consider a standard scholarly commentary on the corvée tension between 1 Kgs 5:13 and 9:22. -->

The judgment of the two women sits in chapter 3, right after Solomon asks God for "an understanding heart to judge thy people" (3:9). In literary terms, it is placed as proof that the request was granted. That placement matters. The story's job in the text is to establish the king's authority. Its last word is not that the child thrived. It is that the people were afraid.

## IV. Making the Problem Go Away

Look at what the court did not do.

It did not ask how the first child died. A woman "overlaid" an infant, and the court took no interest in whether it was an accident. It did not ask how the living child changed hands in the night, which on the winning account was a deliberate theft. It did not ask what would happen to the losing woman, or whether the winner could care for the child. It did not follow up. Two women at the bottom of the social order, described only by their profession, came before the king. They left with a ruling in one hearing, and the king left with a reputation.

That is the clearest sign of what the procedure was for. A court that wanted to know what happened would have asked about the dead child. A court that wanted the dispute gone needed only a decision that nobody could argue with, and fear makes a decision very hard to argue with.

Modern family courts face the same temptation for less dramatic reasons: crowded dockets, closure statistics, and the pressure discussed in [The Church of CYA](/essays/the-church-of-cya/). A case that ends is counted. Whether the ending was right, and how the child did afterward, is not ([priority 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/)).

## V. A Trick That Works Once

Economists took the story seriously enough to give it a name. "King Solomon's dilemma" is a standard problem in mechanism design. How do you give a prize to the person who values it most when everyone can lie about how much they value it?
<!-- CITE: Glazer & Ma, "Efficient Allocation of a 'Prize': King Solomon's Dilemma," Games and Economic Behavior 1(3), 1989. Confirm citation details. -->

The original trick has a fatal weakness. It works only if the lying mother does not see it coming. Once the story spreads, and the text says all Israel heard it, any future impostor knows the right line: "Give her the child." Both women say it, and the court learns nothing. Solomon's method is not a procedure. It is a stunt, and a stunt cannot be repeated.

There is a second weakness. Even the first time, the test does not identify the biological mother. It identifies the woman less willing to see the child harmed. That may be the better custodian, but it is a different question from the one the court claimed to answer. The court asked about maternity and answered with a measure of who would concede.

## VI. The Two Halves of the Story

Strip away the sword, and the judgment of Solomon offers two images of how disputes end.

**The oracle.** A judge with special insight sees through conflicting testimony to the truth, without witnesses or evidence, in a single hearing. "The wisdom of God was in him" (3:28). Nobody appeals.

**The forced concession.** A dispute ends when one side gives up to prevent something worse. The outcome is reached not by finding facts but by making continued conflict intolerable.

Religious tradition carried both images forward, along with a long argument about which is better. The Babylonian Talmud records a debate over whether a judge may push the parties toward compromise. One rabbi says compromise is forbidden: "let the law pierce the mountain." Another says compromise is a duty, citing Zechariah's call to judge with "truth and the judgment of peace." Paul urged early Christians to settle disputes within the community rather than go before outside courts (1 Corinthians 6:1–7). Over centuries, "judgment" and "peace" came to be seen as two distinct virtues, served by two distinct processes.
<!-- CITE: b. Sanhedrin 6b (bitzua / "yikov ha-din et ha-har"); 1 Corinthians 6:1–7. Verify translations. -->

To be precise about causation: modern court-connected mediation did not grow out of 1 Kings. In the United States, it grew mostly out of the alternative dispute resolution movement of the 1970s, including Frank Sander's "multi-door courthouse" proposal at the 1976 Pound Conference. But the vocabulary those reformers inherited, and the public picture of what a judge is supposed to be, came largely through religious transmission of stories like this one. The oracle is the ideal of trial. The forced concession is how settlement works in practice. The split predates the statutes.
<!-- CITE: Sander, "Varieties of Dispute Processing," Pound Conference (1976), 70 F.R.D. 111. -->

## VII. The Sword in the Shadow

In 1979, Robert Mnookin and Lewis Kornhauser described divorce negotiation as "bargaining in the shadow of the law." Parents settle based on what they expect a court would do if they did not. The insight is sound. The question is what the shadow actually looks like to a parent in Ohio.
<!-- CITE: Mnookin & Kornhauser, "Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law: The Case of Divorce," 88 Yale L.J. 950 (1979). -->

For most parents, the shadow of a custody trial is not a wise oracle. It is a sword. It means months or years of delay, retainers, guardian ad litem fees, custody evaluations, a standard schedule that varies by county, and a single judge with wide discretion under ORC § 3109.04 and no presumption to anchor the outcome ([priority 2](/reform/agenda/shared-parenting-presumption/); see also [Two Sets of Books](/essays/two-sets-of-books/), Section IX). The threat is not to cut the child in half. It is to put the child through years of conflict while the family's savings drain away.

Under that threat, settlement runs Solomon's test in reverse. In Solomon's court, the parent who said "don't hurt the child, let her have it" won the child. In a negotiation, the parent who says it gives the child up, and nobody steps in to reverse the concession. The agreement is signed and adopted as an order. The parent most unwilling to see the child harmed by prolonged conflict, or least able to pay for it, accepts the worse arrangement. The parent most willing to keep fighting gets the better one.

This is not mainly about bad mediators, and many are skilled and careful. It is about what the alternative to agreement costs, and who can afford to refuse. [The Standard Schedule](/essays/the-standard-schedule/), Section VI, stages exactly this choice as fiction.

## VIII. Two Tracks That Don't Talk

The dysfunction is not that family law has two tracks. It is that the tracks are sealed off from each other, and neither does the one thing a contested allegation needs.

**Mediation is confidential by design.** Ohio's Uniform Mediation Act, in ORC Chapter 2710, makes most mediation communications privileged. ORC § 3109.052 lets courts order parents into mediation in custody cases and limits what the mediator reports back. Confidentiality has good reasons. It lets people speak candidly. It also means that whatever pressure produced an agreement is invisible to the court that adopts it.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 2710.03 privilege scope and exceptions; ORC 3109.052 mediation provisions, including domestic-violence limits and mediator reporting; Sup.R. 16 requirements for court-connected domestic relations mediation, including DV screening. -->

**Trial rarely arrives.** Most custody cases settle, so the evidentiary hearing that is supposed to test allegations often never happens. Restrictions entered early, sometimes on unadjudicated allegations, set the starting point for negotiation. See [Credibility Follows Evidence](/essays/credibility-follows-evidence/) and [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/).
<!-- CITE: Ohio or national data on settlement rates in contested custody cases; Supreme Court of Ohio statistical reports on domestic relations dispositions. -->

Put those together and an allegation can be neither proven nor disproven. It is priced. It becomes a bargaining chip worth a certain number of overnights, traded away in a room whose contents are privileged, and adopted by a court that never decided whether it was true. The oracle never speaks, because the case settled under the sword. The settlement never informs the court, because what happened in mediation stays there.

There is a final twist in the story's legacy. "Splitting the baby" has become legal slang for a lazy compromise, the ruling that gives each side half because the judge would not decide. The phrase reverses the original, since Solomon never split anything, and it treats shared parenting as a kind of bisection. A child's body cannot be divided. A child's time with two fit parents can be, and children often do better for it. When the metaphor from a story about killing a child is used to discredit equal parenting time, the story is still doing work it should not do.

## IX. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**It's a parable, not a procedure.** Reading a three-thousand-year-old story as institutional design is anachronistic.

**Solomon never intended to harm the child.** The sword was theater, and theater harmed no one.

**Mediation is better for children than trial.** It is faster, cheaper, less adversarial, and lets parents shape their own plan.

**Criticizing a revered religious figure will alienate readers who would otherwise agree.**
{% endcallout %}

**On anachronism.** Fair, as far as it goes. But the story is not just ancient literature. It is the source of the phrase "Solomonic judgment," of "splitting the baby," and of the public picture of a judge who simply knows. We are reading it for what institutions still borrow from it.

**On theater.** The theater only worked because the mother believed it. A system whose fact-finding depends on a credible threat to the child has made the child's welfare the lever. That is true whether the threat is a sword or two years of litigation.

**On mediation.** Agreed, and nothing here proposes abolishing it. The problem is the conditions under which it happens: no findings on threshold allegations, an unpredictable and expensive alternative, and confidentiality that hides how agreement was reached. Fix the conditions and mediation gets better, not worse.

**On religion.** The sharpest critique of Solomon comes from the same scriptures that praise him. Deuteronomy wrote the warning and Kings recorded the violations. Taking those texts seriously means reading all of them, not just the chapter with the sword.

{% callout "protections" %}
Emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31 stays fast and low-barrier. Domestic-violence screening before any court-connected mediation, and the existing limits on ordering mediation where there has been family violence, stay in place and should be strengthened, not weakened. Nothing in this essay suggests that a parent facing abuse should be pushed to negotiate with an abuser.
{% endcallout %}

## X. What Would Fix It

- **Findings before bargaining.** When an allegation is driving a restriction, hold a prompt evidentiary hearing on that threshold question before mediation starts. Then parents bargain in the shadow of facts, not the shadow of the sword ([priority 9](/reform/agenda/evidence-standards-allegations/)).
- **A predictable default.** A rebuttable presumption of substantially shared parenting, with clear exceptions for abuse and danger, makes the alternative to agreement knowable rather than a gamble ([priority 2](/reform/agenda/shared-parenting-presumption/)).
- **Blunt the sword.** Published GAL and evaluator rates, cost caps, limits on repetitive motions, and firm hearing timelines make refusing a bad deal affordable ([priorities 5](/reform/agenda/gal-evaluator-reform/) and [6](/reform/agenda/weaken-conflict-gradient/)).
- **Count what settlement produces.** Keep mediation communications private, but track the outcomes anonymously: how many mediated agreements were reached while a restriction on unadjudicated allegations was in place, how many are later litigated, and how the children fare ([priority 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/)).

## Conclusion

The judgment of Solomon is remembered as wisdom because it ended a dispute that could not otherwise be resolved. Read closely, it ended the dispute without ever resolving it. The dead child went unexamined. The theft went unexamined. The losing woman disappeared from the story, and the child was never heard from again. What the text records is the result that mattered to the throne: the people feared the king.

Family courts do not wield swords, and the people in them are not Solomon. But they inherited his two methods: the hope of an oracle and the reality of a concession extracted under threat. A system that wants to protect children should stop needing either. It should decide the facts that matter, make the alternative to agreement fair and predictable, and then let parents settle the rest in peace.
- **No penalty for declining to concede.** A parent who refuses a proposed agreement, or insists on a hearing, should not be labeled "high conflict" for it without independent findings ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)). See [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), Section III.
