**Thesis.** A support order is a forecast of what a parent can pay. When the forecast rests on income the parent does not actually have, and the law makes the forecast slow to revise and impossible to revise backward, the gap between the order and reality turns into debt. That debt grows fastest among parents with the least income, the enforcement tools used to collect it can shrink that income further, and the children it was meant to support often see little of it.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that parents should be excused from supporting their children, that imputing income is never appropriate, or that most parents who owe arrears are blameless. Some parents hide income or quit work to avoid support, and courts need tools to respond. The claim is about structure: what happens when an estimate is wrong, and how the system is built to notice, or not notice, that it was wrong.
{% endcallout %}

## I. The Number on the Order

Every support order starts with a number. Under Ohio's guidelines in ORC Chapter 3119, the court calculates each parent's income, runs the figures through a statutory schedule, and arrives at a monthly obligation. When a parent's income is clear, from a steady paycheck or a tax return, the math is unremarkable.

When it is not clear, the court may find that a parent is "voluntarily unemployed or underemployed" and add "potential income" to the calculation. Potential income is what the court concludes the parent *could* earn, based on factors such as work history, education, health, and local job availability. The order is then set as if that income exists.
<!-- CITE: ORC 3119.01 (definition of "potential income" and factors); ORC 3119.05. VERIFY current subsection numbering after the 2019 guideline revision (H.B. 366, 132nd GA). -->

From that point, the number on the order is a fact for the purposes of enforcement. It accrues monthly, whether or not the income it assumed ever arrives.

## II. What Imputation Is For

Imputation exists for a real reason, and the essay should say so plainly. Without it, a parent could quit a well-paying job the week before a support hearing, take cash work off the books, or simply decline to work, and the child would bear the cost. Courts need some way to look past a reported income of zero when the zero is a choice.

Used that way, imputation is a fairness tool. It asks a court to weigh evidence about what a parent is realistically able to earn and to hold the parent to it.

The trouble is that the same tool is applied across a very wide range of situations: the parent who quit to avoid support, but also the parent who was laid off, the parent with an untreated health problem, the parent with a record that closes most doors, and the parent in a county where the jobs the court has in mind do not exist. An estimate that is fair for the first can be fiction for the rest. What matters is what the system does when the estimate turns out to be fiction.

## III. The Ratchet

Three features of the law, each defensible on its own, combine into a ratchet that turns upward easily and downward hardly at all.

**Arrears cannot be reduced backward.** Federal law requires every state to treat each missed support payment as a judgment that cannot be retroactively modified. A court may change an order going forward, and in Ohio may make the change effective back to the date a modification request was filed, but not before it. Whatever accrued before the filing stays owed.
<!-- CITE: 42 U.S.C. § 666(a)(9) (the "Bradley Amendment"); Ohio authority on modification relating back to the filing date. -->

**Modification requires the parent to act.** An order does not adjust itself when income falls. The parent has to ask for a review, through the county child support enforcement agency or the court, and show a change of circumstances large enough to qualify. Ohio's threshold is tied to whether a recalculated amount would differ substantially from the existing one.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3119.79 change-of-circumstances threshold (commonly described as a 10 percent deviation) and the CSEA administrative review process under ORC 3119.60 et seq. -->

**The people who most need a review are the least equipped to get one.** A parent who has just lost a job, been hospitalized, or been jailed is not in a good position to file paperwork promptly, and many do not know they have to. Every month of delay is a month of arrears that the Bradley Amendment then makes permanent.

Put together: the obligation can be set high on an estimate, it stays high until the parent successfully petitions, and nothing that accrued in the meantime can be undone. A temporary setback becomes a permanent debt.

## IV. When the Estimate Meets Reality

The predictable failure points are not exotic.

**Job loss.** A parent whose order was set on a prior salary loses that job. The order keeps running at the old rate until a modification is filed and granted. If the court then imputes income near the old salary anyway, on the theory that the parent should be able to find similar work, the gap persists.

**Illness and disability.** A parent who cannot work, but has not yet been formally found disabled, may be treated as able to work in the meantime. Disability determinations take months or longer.

**Incarceration.** For years, many states treated imprisonment as voluntary unemployment, on the reasoning that the parent chose the conduct that led to prison. Support kept accruing at pre-incarceration rates, and parents left prison owing far more than they could repay. A 2016 federal rule now bars state IV-D programs from treating incarceration as voluntary unemployment for purposes of setting or modifying orders. But arrears accrued before the rule, and orders not reviewed since, may still reflect the old approach.
<!-- CITE: 81 Fed. Reg. 93492 (Dec. 20, 2016); 45 C.F.R. § 302.56(c)(3). VERIFY current status of the rule and Ohio's implementing provisions. -->

In each case the order describes a parent who no longer exists, and the law requires the real parent to pay the imaginary one's bill.

## V. Enforcement That Undercuts Itself

Ohio's enforcement tools are listed in [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/): wage withholding, tax-refund intercepts, license suspension, passport denial, contempt, and criminal nonsupport charges under ORC § 2919.21. Against a parent who has money and refuses to pay, these tools work as intended.
<!-- CITE: ORC Chapter 3123 (license suspension); ORC Chapter 2705 (contempt); 42 U.S.C. § 652(k) (passport denial). -->

Against a parent who does not have the money, several of them work in reverse. Suspending a driver's license can make it harder to keep or find a job, especially outside cities with transit. Suspending a professional license removes the very income the order assumed. Jail for contempt ends whatever employment the parent had. Each step can lower the parent's actual income while the imputed figure stays where it was, widening the gap the enforcement was meant to close.

The U.S. Supreme Court addressed part of this in *Turner v. Rogers* (2011). It held that a parent facing jail for civil contempt over unpaid support is not automatically entitled to a lawyer, but that due process requires safeguards to ensure the court actually decides whether the parent is able to pay. Civil contempt is supposed to coerce someone who can comply, not punish someone who cannot.
<!-- CITE: Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431 (2011). Ohio practice on ability-to-pay findings in support contempt. -->

Administrative tools like license suspension often turn on the amount owed rather than on any finding about ability to pay. The structure assumes nonpayment means refusal. Sometimes it does. Often it means there is no money.
The debt also follows the parent into custody court. Failure to pay support, including arrears, is a statutory best-interest factor. Arrears built on imputed income can therefore count against a parent's claim to time with the child. See [Two Sets of Books](/essays/two-sets-of-books/), Section II.


## VI. Who Gets the Money

The usual defense of aggressive enforcement is that the money goes to children. Often it does. But not always, and not as much as the arrears totals suggest.

First, some support owed to families that received public assistance is assigned to the state to reimburse those benefits, rather than paid to the child's household. Second, a large share of arrears nationally is owed by parents with little or no reported income, and much of it is unlikely ever to be collected. A balance that will never be paid does not feed anyone. It sits on the books, accrues, and justifies continued enforcement.
<!-- CITE: 42 U.S.C. § 657 (distribution and assignment); Sorensen, Sousa & Schaner, "Assessing Child Support Arrears in Nine Large States and the Nation" (Urban Institute, 2007); OCSE data on arrears and collectibility. Ohio-specific figures if available. -->

Meanwhile, the measures the system is rewarded for are collections and enforcement activity, as discussed in [The Church of CYA](/essays/the-church-of-cya/). A program measured by dollars collected has little reason to measure how much of its caseload is chasing money that does not exist, or how many parents drop out of the formal economy, and out of their children's lives, under debt they cannot pay.

There is also a connection to parenting time. A parent buried in arrears may avoid the court, the agency, and sometimes the child's household, because every contact carries a risk of enforcement. That is the kind of risk management described in [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/). The support order and the relationship it was supposed to accompany pull in opposite directions. As in [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/), money ends up deciding who stays in a child's life.

## VII. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**Imputation exists because some parents game the system.** Without it, any parent could avoid support by choosing not to work.

**Arrears are owed to children.** Forgiving or reducing them takes money from the people the system exists to protect.

**Parents can file for modification.** The process is available, and those who don't use it have themselves to blame.
{% endcallout %}

**On gaming.** Agreed, and nothing here removes imputation. The proposal is that imputation rest on evidence about the specific parent and the specific labor market, stated in written findings, and that it be reviewed when the facts it relied on change. A parent who is hiding income should still be held to what they can earn. A parent who has lost a job should not be held indefinitely to one they no longer have.

**On children's money.** Support that can be collected should be collected, and nothing here forgives arrears owed by parents who can pay. But an arrears figure built on income that never existed is not money the child is losing. It is money the child was never going to get. Treating it as real can drive the paying parent out of formal work and out of the child's life, which costs the child more than the uncollectible balance is worth.

**On modification.** The process exists, but it puts the burden of noticing an error on the party least able to act on it, at the moment they are least able to act. Systems that depend on people in crisis completing paperwork on time will fail predictably, and the Bradley Amendment makes each failure permanent. Automatic review triggers would not change who pays. They would change how fast the order reflects the truth.

{% callout "protections" %}
Enforcement against parents who can pay and refuse to stays fully in force. Nothing in this essay weakens wage withholding, tax intercepts, or contempt for willful nonpayment, and nothing proposes forgiving support owed by a parent with the means to pay it. The proposals change how orders are estimated, reviewed, and enforced when the parent's actual income does not match the order.
{% endcallout %}

## VIII. What Would Fix It

- **Imputation on evidence, in writing.** A finding of voluntary unemployment or underemployment should state the evidence, the income figure, and the basis for believing that income is actually available to this parent in this labor market.
- **Automatic review triggers.** Notice of job loss, incarceration, hospitalization, or a disability application should start a review without waiting for the parent to file, so that the date a modification can relate back to is set by the event, not the paperwork.
- **Ability-to-pay findings before coercive enforcement.** License suspension, contempt, and criminal referral should require a finding that the parent can pay, consistent with *Turner v. Rogers*. Tools that remove earning capacity should be the last resort, not the first.
- **Pay the child first.** Where support is assigned to the state, Ohio should pass through as much as federal rules allow to the child's household.
- **Enforce both halves of the order.** If support is enforced administratively, parenting time should be too ([priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/)).
- **Count the uncollectible.** Report arrears by obligor income band, how many orders rest on imputed income, how often imputed orders are later modified downward, and how much of the arrears balance is realistically collectible ([priorities 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/) and [12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/)).

## Conclusion

A support order is a promise the state makes on a child's behalf: that a parent's real resources will be shared with the child. When the order is built on income the parent does not have, it stops being that promise. It becomes a debt that grows on schedule, that the law forbids anyone from correcting backward, and that the state's own tools can make harder to pay.

None of that requires anyone to act in bad faith. It requires only an estimate that is easy to make, hard to revise, and impossible to undo, enforced by a system that counts what it collects and never counts what it cannot. Children are owed support from parents who can pay it. They are also owed a system honest enough to tell the difference between a parent who won't pay and one who can't.
