**Thesis.** A legal system shows its real priorities where its rules disagree with each other. Ohio family law repeatedly answers the same question two ways: whether money and parenting are connected, whether a child's voice matters, whether family matters are private, whether credentials matter. The answer each time is whichever one makes the institution's job easier. These are not isolated drafting errors. Together they form a pattern, and the pattern is the subject of this essay.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that the people who wrote or apply these rules are hypocrites in any personal sense, that any judge or magistrate acts in bad faith, or that every rule discussed here lacks a legitimate purpose. Most of them have one, and each section says what it is. "Hypocrisy" here means something narrower: an institution applying opposite principles to the same question depending on which principle serves it. "Absurdity" means a rule whose results no one would defend if they were stated plainly.
{% endcallout %}

## I. A Standard for the Charge

"Hypocrisy" is easy to throw and hard to earn, so it is worth defining before using it.

An individual is a hypocrite when they hold others to a standard they excuse in themselves. An institution can do something similar without any one person intending it. It can adopt a principle in one statute and the opposite principle in another, and then apply whichever fits the case in front of it. Each rule may be defensible alone. The problem shows up only when you read them side by side and ask what the system actually believes.

The test used below is simple. For each contradiction:

1. State both rules in plain terms.
2. State the best reason for each.
3. Ask whether any single principle explains both. If none does except "whichever is easier to administer" or "whichever protects the institution," the charge is earned.

The eight examples below are specific Ohio provisions or practices. None requires a conspiracy. All of them can be fixed by statute or rule.

## II. Money Counts Against You in Custody. Lost Time Counts for Nothing in Support.

Ohio's custody statute lists the factors a court weighs in deciding a child's best interest. One of them asks whether either parent has failed to make all required child support payments, including arrearages. Unpaid support is, by statute, evidence about who should raise the child.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.04(F)(1)(g) wording on failure to make child support payments, including arrearages. -->

Now run the logic the other way. A parent who is denied court-ordered parenting time for months cannot raise that denial as a defense to paying support. The two obligations are legally independent. Courts say so routinely, and with good reason: a child should not go without food because the adults are fighting over the calendar.
<!-- CITE: Ohio appellate authority holding that interference with parenting time is not a defense to nonpayment of support, and vice versa. -->

To be fair, the custody statute also lists a factor about whether a residential parent has continuously and willfully denied the other parent's parenting time. On paper, both failures count in custody.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.04(F)(1)(i). -->

The asymmetry is in enforcement. Unpaid support is detected by a state agency, recorded automatically, and pursued with wage withholding, license suspension, and contempt, at no cost to the parent who is owed. Denied parenting time is detected by no one. It is recorded only if the parent who lost it pays a lawyer to file a motion and proves it months later. One failure arrives at the custody hearing as an official arrears printout. The other arrives as a parent's word, if it arrives at all. The printout may itself rest on income the parent never earned; see [Income You Don't Have](/essays/income-you-dont-have/).

So the principle is not "money and parenting are separate," because money enters the custody analysis. It is not "money and parenting are connected" either, because parenting never enters the support analysis. The working principle is that the obligation the state collects on is the one it counts. See [priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/), [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), Section V, and [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/), Section III.

## III. The Ninetieth Night

Ohio's 2019 child support guideline revision added a parenting-time adjustment. When a court-ordered schedule gives the paying parent ninety or more overnights a year, the obligation is reduced by ten percent, and the court may deviate further.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3119.051 (90-overnight threshold, 10 percent adjustment), as enacted by H.B. 366, 132nd GA, effective 2019. Confirm current text and any later amendment. -->

The adjustment recognizes something true: a parent who houses and feeds a child for a quarter of the year is already spending money on that child. The problem is the cliff. Eighty-nine overnights earn nothing, and ninety earn a ten percent reduction. Nothing about a child's needs changes between those two nights, but the price does.

A cliff like that does two things. It gives the paying parent a financial reason to fight for the ninetieth night, and it gives the receiving parent a financial reason to fight against it. The schedule negotiation, which is supposed to be about the child's week, becomes partly a negotiation about money. A parent who asks for one more overnight can then be accused of doing it for the discount. Sometimes the accusation is true. The statute made it plausible either way.

A smooth adjustment that scales with actual overnights would recognize the same truth without creating a line worth litigating over. The current design turns one particular night of a child's year into a financial event, which is the kind of outcome nobody would choose if it were stated plainly.

## IV. Old Enough to Be Tried, Too Young to Be Heard

Under Ohio's juvenile bindover statute, a child as young as fourteen can be transferred to adult court for certain serious offenses. For some offenses transfer is mandatory once age and probable cause are established. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds face broader mandatory transfer. The law, in other words, is willing to treat some teenagers as capable of adult judgment and adult responsibility.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 2152.10 and 2152.12 (mandatory and discretionary transfer ages and offense categories). -->

In a custody case, the same sixteen-year-old's view about which parent to live with is one factor among many. The court may interview the child in chambers. It is not required to follow the child's wishes, and Ohio sets no age at which those wishes become controlling. The child is usually not a party to the case and has no lawyer of their own unless the court appoints one.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.04(B) (in-camera interview; discretionary weight); Ohio law on appointment of counsel for children in custody cases, as distinct from a GAL. -->

There are good reasons for caution about children's preferences. Children can be pressured, coached, or bribed with a later bedtime. Making a child choose between parents can itself be harmful. Those reasons are real.

But they do not explain the contradiction. If a sixteen-year-old is mature enough to be held to adult standards for the worst decision of their life, the state can hardly claim they are too immature for their considered view of their own home to carry real weight. The working principle appears to be that a teenager's judgment counts when it supports more state control and is discounted when it would limit it. A presumption that the stated preference of an older child carries substantial weight, rebuttable by evidence of coaching or danger, would resolve the contradiction without giving children a veto. See [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), Section VI.

## V. Private When It Protects the Court, Public When It Shames a Parent

Family proceedings are among the most closed in Ohio's court system. Many juvenile records are confidential. Custody evaluations and GAL reports are typically restricted. The reason given is privacy: children and families deserve protection from public exposure of their worst years. That reason is sound.

Yet the same system has, at times, published names and photographs of parents owing support. Some county child support agencies have run "most wanted" style lists, posters, or press releases about delinquent obligors. License suspensions are entered in state databases, and passport denials in federal ones.
<!-- VERIFY: current or recent Ohio county CSEA practice of publishing delinquent-obligor lists or posters; ODJFS policy on publication. Do not name a county without a primary source. -->

Meanwhile, the system publishes almost nothing about itself. As discussed in [The Church of CYA](/essays/the-church-of-cya/), Ohio produces no systematic county-by-county data on how parenting time is allocated, how often GAL recommendations are adopted, how long emergency restrictions last, or how children fare afterward.
<!-- CITE: Supreme Court of Ohio Court Statistical Reports (what is and is not reported). -->

So privacy is a strong principle when the subject is the court's own performance, and a weak one when the subject is a parent's debt. A consistent rule would run the other way. Individual families would be protected, and aggregate institutional outcomes would be published. See [priorities 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/) and [12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/).

## VI. Same Child, Different Court

In most Ohio counties, a custody dispute between married parents goes to the domestic relations court as part of a divorce or dissolution. A custody dispute between parents who never married goes to the juvenile court. The question is identical in both: where should this child live, and how should the parents share time? But the courthouse, the magistrates, the local rules, and sometimes the default parenting schedule can all differ, depending on whether the parents ever signed a marriage license.
<!-- VERIFY: county-by-county jurisdictional split (some counties combine domestic relations and juvenile divisions); confirm ORC 2151.23 and ORC 3105 jurisdiction provisions. -->

The starting points differ too. Under ORC § 3109.042, an unmarried mother is the sole residential parent and legal custodian until a court says otherwise. A father who signs an acknowledgment of paternity takes on a support obligation the moment it becomes final. He gets no enforceable parenting time until he files in juvenile court and asks. A married father starts as an equal legal custodian, with no petition required.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.042; ORC 3111.25 and related sections on the effect of an acknowledgment of paternity. Check for any amendment allowing parenting-time terms in the acknowledgment process. -->

The best-interest standard is supposed to be about the child. The child had no say in whether their parents married. If the standard really governs, it is hard to explain why the child's starting position, and the court that decides their life, should turn on a decision the adults made before the child existed. The working principle is administrative history: the two courts grew up separately, and the law never reconciled them. A child should not inherit a jurisdictional accident. See [priority 1](/reform/agenda/presumption-of-standing/).

## VII. An Hour Late Is a Crime. Three States Away Is a Notice.

A parent who keeps a child past the court-ordered return time, knowing they have no right to, risks a charge of interference with custody under ORC § 2919.23. Police can be called to the door over an exchange that runs late.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 2919.23 elements and degree of offense; confirm how often it is charged against residential versus nonresidential parents, if any data exists. -->

A residential parent who plans to move generally must file a notice of intent to relocate with the court. The court may then schedule a hearing to revise the parenting-time schedule. Unless the decree or local rule says otherwise, the statute requires notice, not permission. A move across the state, or out of it, can reduce the other parent's time from every weekend to a few visits a year. That is a larger loss of contact than any late exchange, and it can be accomplished largely through paperwork.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.051(G) relocation-notice provisions and the court's authority on revision; local rules requiring court approval before relocation; ORC Chapter 3127 on interstate jurisdiction after a move. -->

Relocation has legitimate reasons: a job, family support, escape from danger. Rigid relocation bans would trap parents and sometimes endanger them. The point is not that moving should be forbidden. It is that the law treats a small, temporary deprivation of the residential parent's time as potentially criminal, and a large, permanent deprivation of the other parent's time as an administrative filing. A principle that measured harm by its size, rather than by which parent caused it, would make the residential parent show before the move that the new schedule preserves a meaningful relationship, except where danger is shown.

## VIII. Twelve Hours of Training

A guardian ad litem in an Ohio custody case investigates the family, interviews the child, and makes a recommendation that courts often weigh heavily. Under the Rules of Superintendence, a GAL must complete an initial training course and annual continuing education. The required hours are counted in the low double digits for initial training and single digits each year after.
<!-- VERIFY: Sup.R. 48 series (as reorganized in 2021) for current pre-service and continuing training hours; commonly described as 12 hours initial and 3 hours annually. -->

For comparison, Ohio requires far more instruction before a person may cut hair for money. Barber and cosmetology licenses require training measured in the hundreds or thousands of hours.
<!-- VERIFY: Ohio barber and cosmetology licensing hour requirements (ORC 4709, 4713) before publishing a specific figure. -->

The comparison is meant to sting, but the point is about consistency, not barbers. Many GALs are experienced attorneys, and many do careful work. The rules also let parties cross-examine a GAL and object to a report. Still, the same court system that applies demanding standards to expert testimony, and requires a doctorate for a custody evaluator, allows a recommendation about where a child lives to come from someone whose family-specific training may fit into two workdays. GALs are often paid by the parents they evaluate, and complaints about them run through the courts that appoint them.

If the recommendation matters enough to shape a child's life, the training and accountability should match its weight. If it does not matter that much, courts should say so and weigh it accordingly. The system currently treats the recommendation as weighty and the preparation as minor. See [priority 5](/reform/agenda/gal-evaluator-reform/). [The Standard Schedule](/essays/the-standard-schedule/) imagines a GAL appointment from the side of the parent being evaluated.

## IX. Shared Parenting, If Someone Asks

Ohio courts can enter a shared-parenting decree. Under the statute, though, the court may approve shared parenting only when at least one parent requests it and a plan is filed. If neither parent files, or the court rejects the plans, it allocates parental rights primarily to one parent. Ohio has no presumption of shared parenting. Bills to create one have been introduced repeatedly and, as of this writing, none has passed.
<!-- VERIFY: ORC 3109.04(A)(1)-(2), (D)(1) on shared-parenting requests and plans; status of shared-parenting presumption bills in the 135th and 136th General Assemblies. -->

Consider what that default does. In the cases where parents cooperate, they usually agree on a schedule themselves. The statute's default therefore matters most in the cases where they do not. There, the absence of a shared plan leads toward one residential parent and one visitor. The more the parents fight, the more likely the result is winner-take-all. A rule meant for high-conflict families ends up rewarding conflict. [Splitting the Baby](/essays/splitting-the-baby/), Section VII, describes how that unpredictable default shapes the settlements most parents reach without a trial.

Meanwhile, courts routinely say that both parents' involvement is good for children, and the best-interest factors ask which parent is more likely to facilitate the other's relationship with the child. The stated value is two involved parents. The procedural default, when no plan is filed, is one. A rebuttable presumption of substantially shared parenting, overcome by evidence of danger or unfitness, would match the default to the stated value. See [priority 2](/reform/agenda/shared-parenting-presumption/).

## X. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**These are separate rules written for separate purposes.** Calling them hypocritical assumes they should share a principle, when legislatures routinely balance different goals in different statutes.

**Every rule here protects someone real.** Support independence protects children's food and housing. Discounting children's wishes protects them from pressure. Relocation flexibility protects parents fleeing abuse.

**Listing contradictions is easy. Writing a coherent system is hard.** Critics who demand consistency rarely offer one.
{% endcallout %}

**On separate purposes.** That is fair as far as it goes, and each section above states the purpose of each rule. But these rules do not govern separate subjects. They govern the same family, often in the same case, sometimes at the same hearing. When a parent's arrears are entered against him in custody, and his lost weekends are excluded from support, he is not experiencing two statutes. He is experiencing one system with two sets of books.

**On protection.** Agreed, and none of the proposals below removes those protections. Support stays independent of parenting time for purposes of payment. Children's wishes stay subject to inquiry into coaching. Parents fleeing danger keep every existing route out, including emergency orders. The argument is that a protection for the exceptional case should not quietly become the rule for every case.

**On coherence.** Also fair, which is why each section ends with a specific fix. None requires redesigning Ohio family law from scratch. Most bring one rule into line with a principle the system already claims elsewhere.

{% callout "protections" %}
Emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31 remains fast and low-barrier. Nothing here weakens support enforcement against parents who can pay, limits a parent's ability to relocate away from danger, or requires a child to choose between parents. The proposals change how existing rules are applied when no danger has been found.
{% endcallout %}

## XI. What Would Fix It

- **Smooth the cliff.** Replace the ninety-overnight threshold with an adjustment that scales with actual parenting time, so no single night is worth litigating over.
- **Give older children's views real weight.** Above a defined age, a child's stated preference should carry substantial weight, rebuttable by findings of coaching, pressure, or danger, and the child should be able to request counsel of their own.
- **Reverse the privacy rule.** Protect individual families. Publish aggregate outcomes for courts, GALs, and agencies ([priorities 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/) and [12](/reform/agenda/records-transparency/)).
- **One standard for every child.** Give legally established parents the same starting position regardless of marital status, and apply consistent rules whichever court hears the case ([priority 1](/reform/agenda/presumption-of-standing/)).
- **Measure relocation by its effect.** Require a showing, before a long-distance move, of how the child's relationship with the other parent will be preserved, with an exception for documented danger.
- **Train GALs to match their weight.** Raise training and accountability standards, publish appointment and fee data, and route complaints outside the appointing court ([priority 5](/reform/agenda/gal-evaluator-reform/)).
- **Make the default match the value.** Adopt a rebuttable presumption of substantially shared parenting, with clear exceptions for abuse and danger ([priority 2](/reform/agenda/shared-parenting-presumption/)).
- **Treat objection as information.** A parent who points out that two rules contradict each other is not showing "high conflict." They are reading the code ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)).

## Conclusion

Every legal system has rough edges where statutes written in different decades meet. That alone is not a scandal. What makes Ohio's contradictions worth naming is their direction. They do not cut randomly. Again and again, the rule that applies is the one that is easier to collect on, easier to administer, or easier to defend afterward.

Money counts in custody, but time does not count in support. A teenager's judgment is adult in a criminal court and childish in a family court. Privacy shields the institution and exposes the parent. A late exchange can be a crime, while a move across the country is a filing.

None of this requires anyone to act in bad faith. It only requires that nobody be responsible for reading the rules side by side. Families do read them side by side, because they live under all of them at once. A system that asks parents to accept its authority should at least be willing to apply its own principles consistently.
- **One set of books.** Track denied parenting time with the same administrative seriousness as missed support, so both arrive at a custody hearing as records rather than one record and one accusation ([priority 3](/reform/agenda/tie-obligations-to-standing/)). Court-ordered co-parenting apps could help here, but as generally designed they do not; see [Every Word an Exhibit](/essays/every-word-an-exhibit/), Section V.
