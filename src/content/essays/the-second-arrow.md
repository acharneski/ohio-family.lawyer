Call or text [988](tel:988), any time. If you or a child are in danger, call [911](tel:911). This essay is reflection, not treatment and not legal advice. Getting help is a strength, not an admission of anything. See [Support](/support/).
{% endcallout %}

**Thesis.** A father inside a long, contested custody case is struck twice. The first blow is the case itself: the orders, the allegations, the lost weekends. He did not choose it and often cannot stop it. The second blow is what he does to himself afterward, in anger, rumination, drink, and words that end up as exhibits. Two very different traditions, Roman Stoicism and early Buddhism, agree that the second blow is the one he has some power over. Surviving it is not the same as accepting the first.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that injustice should be accepted quietly, that a father should stop contesting orders he believes are wrong, or that philosophy can replace a lawyer, a clinician, or reform. It does not offer religious instruction, and it does not claim that either tradition endorses any particular legal position. The conversation is invented. Marcus Aurelius and the Buddha never met, and the words given to them here are paraphrases and extensions of their recorded teachings, not quotations unless marked.
{% endcallout %}

## A Note on the Conversation

Why these two? Because each one knew something specific about this situation.

Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome from 161 to 180, wrote the notebook we call the *Meditations* largely while on military campaign along the Danube, far from home. He and his wife Faustina had many children, and a number of them died young. He also spent a great deal of his reign hearing cases, and Roman sources credit him with reforms to the guardianship of minors.
<!-- VERIFY: number of children and childhood deaths (commonly cited as 13 or 14 children); Book 1 and Book 2 campaign headings ("among the Quadi," "at Carnuntum"); Cassius Dio 72(71) on Marcus's judicial care; Historia Augusta, Marcus 10 on the praetor tutelaris. Note that the Historia Augusta is an unreliable source. -->

Siddhattha Gotama, the Buddha, is remembered in later tradition as having left his wife and newborn son, Rahula, to seek awakening. Years later Rahula joined him, and some of the Buddha's most practical teachings were given to that son. A teacher who once walked away from his child, and who then spent years teaching that child how to act, has standing on this subject that a gentler biography would not give him.
<!-- CITE: Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta (MN 61); later commentarial accounts of the departure. Note that the "left on the night of the birth" detail is from later sources, not the earliest suttas. -->

Neither man faced an Ohio magistrate. Both faced the problem underneath: what to do with a mind when the world has taken something you love and you cannot get it back by force.

## I. The Waiting Room

Picture a hallway outside a courtroom in a county courthouse. A father sits on a bench. His case has been open for fourteen months. An interim order gives him alternating weekends. An allegation made early in the case has never been heard on the merits. The guardian ad litem's report is due next month. He has spent his savings. He has not slept well since the first motion.

Two strangers sit down on either side of him.

**Marcus.** You are waiting for someone else to decide what happens to your children.

**The father.** For fourteen months.

**Marcus.** I spent years waiting on the Danube for news of mine. Some of it was the worst news a father can get. I will not tell you it is easy. I will tell you what I told myself, because I needed to hear it more than once.

**The Buddha.** And I will tell you what I learned, which is not always the same thing. We should be honest with him where we disagree.

## II. What Is Up to You

**Marcus.** Begin by sorting. The magistrate's decision is not yours. The other parent's choices are not yours. The guardian's report is not yours. What is yours is narrower than you would like: your judgments, your words, your conduct today. When I was pained by something outside me, I wrote that the pain came less from the thing than from my verdict about it, and that the verdict I could revise.
<!-- VERIFY: Meditations 8.47 (pain from judgment about external things), against a named translation (Hays, Hammond, or Long). -->

**The father.** That sounds like being told the injustice is in my head.

**Marcus.** No. The order is real. The lost weekend is real. I am not asking you to call a wrong thing right. I am asking you not to add a second wrong to it with your own hands.

**The Buddha.** I describe this with an image. A person struck by an arrow feels pain. Then he strikes himself with a second arrow: he grieves, he rages, he replays the moment, he tells himself it will never end. Now he has two wounds. The first arrow came from outside. The second is the one you can learn to stop firing.
<!-- CITE: Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6 (the two arrows). -->

**The father.** The first arrow is still in me.

**The Buddha.** Yes. Nobody here will pretend it isn't. Pull out what you can. Get the help you need for the wound. But notice how many arrows you have fired since the first one, and where your hand is right now.

This is the core of what both traditions offer, and it is easy to mishear. Neither says the first blow does not matter. Both say the first blow is not the only one, and that a person in pain can be taught to stop doubling it. See [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/) on why the pain of the first arrow is often an accurate response, not a disorder.

## III. Anger

**The father.** I am angry all the time.

**Marcus.** Of course you are. I began my mornings by reminding myself that I would meet people who were meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, and dishonest that day. Not to excuse them. To be ready, so their conduct would not surprise me into becoming like them. The best revenge, I wrote, is not to become like the one who wronged you.
<!-- VERIFY: Meditations 2.1 (morning preparation) and 6.6 (best revenge), against a named translation. -->

**The Buddha.** I called anger a thing with a poisoned root and a honeyed tip. The tip is sweet. It feels like strength, like being on the side of justice. The root is what it does to you over months. Hatred is not ended by hatred. It ends by its absence. That is an old law.
<!-- CITE: SN 1.71 and parallels ("poisoned root, honeyed tip"); Dhammapada 5. -->

**The father.** So I'm supposed to stop caring that this is unfair.

**The Buddha.** No. Anger and the perception of unfairness are different things. You can see clearly that something is unjust, and say so, and act against it, without letting anger hold the pen. In fact you will see more clearly. Anger narrows the eye.

**Marcus.** And in your situation anger is not only a private poison. It is evidence. A raised voice at an exchange, a furious message at midnight, a face that shows contempt in a hearing: any of these can be written down by someone whose job is to write things down. I do not say this is fair. I say it is the terrain. A soldier who curses the river does not cross it.

This connects to a structural problem the site has written about directly in [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), Section III. Ohio's best-interest factors ask which parent is more likely to facilitate the other's relationship with the child, and in practice a parent who objects strongly can be described as "high conflict" ([priority 10](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/)). That is a defect in the system. Until it is fixed, a father has to fight it without supplying its evidence.

## IV. The Mirror

**The Buddha.** When my son was young, I taught him with a mirror. What is a mirror for, I asked him. For looking at yourself, he said. Then before you act, I told him, look at the act the same way. Will this harm me, or another, or both? If so, don't. While you are doing it, look again. If you see it is harmful, stop. After you have done it, look once more, and learn.
<!-- CITE: Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta (MN 61). -->

**The father.** That's what you taught your son.

**The Buddha.** It is what I would teach any father. You write messages to the other parent. You write emails to your lawyer. You speak at exchanges. Every one of those can be held up to the mirror before it leaves you. I also taught that good speech is true, beneficial, well-timed, gentle, and spoken with goodwill. If it fails one of those, wait.
<!-- CITE: Vaca Sutta (AN 5.198); Abhaya Sutta (MN 58) on true and beneficial speech that may still be unwelcome. -->

**Marcus.** Your court has a simpler test, and it agrees with him. Write every message as though the magistrate will read it, because she may. Short, factual, about the children, with no commentary on the other parent's character. If it would embarrass you on a screen in a courtroom, it is not finished.

**The father.** Sometimes the true thing needs to be said.

**The Buddha.** Then say it, at the right time, in the right place, to the right person. Often that person is your lawyer, not the other parent. The right place is often a filing, not a text. Truth spoken at the wrong time can still do harm, and the harm can land on you and the children.
When a court orders parents to communicate through a co-parenting app, Marcus's test stops being hypothetical, because every message is kept and can be exported for court. See [Every Word an Exhibit](/essays/every-word-an-exhibit/).


## V. Where They Disagree

Here the two traditions part ways, and the difference matters to a father.

**Marcus.** I believe in roles. A man is a citizen, a son, a husband, a father, and each role carries duties he does not get to resign because they became painful. You are a father. Act as one, in whatever space you are given. Two hours with your children are two hours of the role, fully done. Do not let anyone, including your own despair, talk you out of it.

**The Buddha.** I would be careful there. When a person says "this is my child, this is my role, this is who I am," he builds a self out of something that can be taken away by a court order. Then each restriction feels like annihilation. I would loosen the grip on "mine" so the loss of time does not become the loss of everything.

**Marcus.** Loosen it too far and he walks away. Forgive me, but you did.

**The Buddha.** I did. I left my son when he was an infant. Later traditions tell it as a noble departure, and perhaps for the path it was. For Rahula it was a father gone. When he came to me as a boy, the first thing I owed him was presence and instruction. So hear me plainly: letting go of clinging does not mean letting go of your children. It means letting go of the demand that the court, the other parent, and the calendar give you back the self you had. Your children need your presence, not your possession.

**The father.** Then which of you is right?

**Marcus.** Both, in different hours. Hold the role as a duty. Do not let it be the whole of you.

**The Buddha.** Agreed. Be the father fully in the time you have. In the time you do not have, be a person who can survive until the next time.

That resolution matches advice from the clinical side of this site: rebuild identity as a worker, friend, or neighbor, without abandoning parenthood but without letting it carry the whole self. It also carries a warning both men would endorse. A father who reads "let go" as "give up" has misheard both of them. Disengaging from a hostile process may sometimes be necessary. Disengaging from children who are alive and waiting for him is a different act.

## VI. Your Children Are Alive

**The Buddha.** There is a story told about a young mother named Kisa Gotami. Her child died, and she carried his body from house to house asking for medicine. I told her to bring me a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, and she understood that loss comes to every house.
<!-- CITE: Kisa Gotami story, Dhammapada commentary and Therigatha commentary. Note that it is commentarial, not from the earliest suttas. -->

**The father.** I've heard that story. I don't think it applies.

**The Buddha.** It doesn't, and that is why I tell it. Her child was dead. Yours are not. Many fathers in your place begin to mourn living children as though they were gone. They grieve the relationship as already over, and then act as though it is. Do not do that. The time you are losing is real loss, and you may grieve it. The children are not lost.

**Marcus.** I buried children. I would have given the empire for one more ordinary afternoon with any of them. Your afternoons are rationed, and unjustly perhaps. But they exist. My teacher Epictetus said that when you kiss your child, you should remember he is mortal. It sounds cruel. It was meant to make the kiss complete instead of distracted.
<!-- VERIFY: Meditations 11.34, quoting Epictetus. -->

**The Buddha.** Everything changes. That includes the case. Your children are growing every week, and that hurts, because time with them is passing. It is also true that the order will not last forever, that the children will one day decide for themselves whom to see, and that what they will remember is not the docket. They will remember how you were when you were with them.

**Marcus.** I could not even control the character of my own son, and he went on to rule Rome. I did my part. A father's part is to do his part, and it has to be enough, because there is nothing else on offer.

## VII. Two Notebooks

**Marcus.** My notebook was written to myself. I never meant anyone else to read it.

**The father.** I've been told to keep a journal.

**Marcus.** Keep two, and know which is which. One is a record: dates, times, what happened, who was there, what was said. Plain facts, written the same day, with no adjectives. That one is for your memory, and for your lawyer if it is useful. The other is where you argue with yourself, as I did. Where you sort what is yours from what is not, and remind yourself what kind of father you intend to be today.

**The Buddha.** And be careful where the second one lives.

**Marcus.** Yes. A private notebook is only private if no one can demand it. Ask your lawyer what can be requested in your case before you write your worst hours on paper or on a device. Some fathers do their arguing aloud, on a walk, or with a counselor whose confidentiality they have asked about first.

The site's [Start here](/start/) page describes keeping a factual log. On whether reflective writing or therapy records could be reached in litigation, see [priority 14](/reform/agenda/treatment-is-not-testimony/), and ask your own attorney. The rules depend on the case, the court, and what has been ordered.

## VIII. Instructions for Surviving

By the end of the hour the father asks for something he can carry out of the building. What follows is the list the two of them might give him. It is general, not advice for any specific case. Use your own clinician and attorney for that.

- **Prepare in the morning.** Before an exchange or a hearing, take two minutes to expect difficulty. Name what you will probably face and how you intend to act when it comes. Being ready is not the same as being resigned.
- **Hold up the mirror before you send.** Ask whether a message is true, useful, timely, and kind enough to be read aloud in court. If not, wait a day. Most messages that need to be sent today are about logistics.
- **Stop firing the second arrow.** When you catch yourself replaying a hearing for the fortieth time, say so to yourself, and return to something in front of you: work, a walk, a meal, sleep.
- **Keep the body steady.** Sleep, food, movement. Marcus wrote at length about getting out of bed when he did not want to. That was not a small thing to him, and it is not a small thing now.
- **Keep away from intoxicants.** Both traditions counsel against them. Here there is also a practical reason. Under chronic stress the pull toward numbing is strong, and in a custody case its costs are counted twice. Treat it as a hazard to manage, with someone you are accountable to.
- **Find one good friend.** When a disciple suggested that good friendship was half of the spiritual life, the Buddha corrected him: it is the whole of it. One trustworthy person who does not minimize the injury, and does not demand a show of strength, is worth more than a crowd of sympathizers.
<!-- CITE: Upaddha Sutta (SN 45.2). -->
- **Wish your children well when you cannot see them.** A simple practice: on nights without them, picture each child and wish them safety, health, and ease. It costs nothing, creates no record, and keeps the bond active in you. Some people extend the same wish, later, to the other parent. That is not required, and it can wait.
- **Do justice without needing the outcome.** Justice was one of the four Stoic virtues, not an optional extra. Contest what is wrong through the proper channels, with your lawyer, with evidence. Then let the result be what it is, and keep acting well after it.
- **Look from above.** Marcus imagined viewing his troubles from a great height: the whole city, the whole century. It is not meant to make your children small. It is meant to make the motion hearing small, and your children large.
<!-- VERIFY: Meditations 9.30 and related "view from above" passages. -->
- **Get care.** Therapy, a physician, a support group, a crisis line. Both men would have counted this as practice, not weakness.

## IX. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**This is quietism.** Telling a wronged father to manage his own mind lets the institution off the hook.

**These are a Roman emperor and a prince.** They had power, wealth, and choices this father does not have.

**This flattens two very different traditions.** Stoicism and Buddhism disagree about the self, about nature, and about the purpose of practice. Pairing them as one self-help program distorts both.

**A calm father may look to the court like a father who has stopped caring.**
{% endcallout %}

**On quietism.** The objection is serious, and it is why this essay sits next to reform proposals, not instead of them. Neither tradition asks the father to call the injustice just. Marcus was a working judge and legislator, and Stoic justice is active. What both traditions add is that a person can contest a wrong for years without being destroyed by the contest, and that contesting it well usually requires that he not be destroyed.

**On privilege.** True, and worth stating. Marcus never had to choose between a retainer and rent. But both men faced losses that power could not prevent, and the teachings that survive are the ones about those losses. The advice above costs nothing, which is part of why it is offered.

**On flattening.** Fair. The two do disagree, and Section V is where the disagreement is allowed to show. A father does not need to resolve a two-thousand-year argument about the self. He needs whatever helps him get to the next exchange intact, and the overlap between these traditions is large enough to be useful.

**On appearances.** Calm is not indifference, and a well-kept record, prompt compliance, measured communication, and steady presence with the children are the opposite of not caring. If a court reads composure as apathy and distress as instability, that is the trap described in [The Function, Not the Failure](/essays/the-function-not-the-failure/). A father cannot fully escape it, but composure with clear, documented engagement is the safer side of it. Talk with your attorney about how to show the court what you are doing.

{% callout "protections" %}
Nothing here suggests that a parent should stay silent about danger to a child, ignore an order, or negotiate with someone who has been violent. Emergency protection under ORC § 3113.31 stays fast and low-barrier. If you believe a child is in danger, call 911 or your county children services agency, and talk to your attorney.
{% endcallout %}

## X. What the Court Owes Him

The conversation ends with a question the father asks Marcus, the only one of the three who ever sat in judgment.

**The father.** If you were the magistrate, what would you do?

**Marcus.** I would hear the allegation and decide it, quickly, instead of letting it stand untested while a father's children grow. I would write down why any restriction exists and when it will be reviewed. I would not count a man's protest against him without asking whether he was right. And I would want to know, years later, how the children turned out.

He is describing, in other words, the reforms this site argues for: [findings before restrictions continue](/reform/agenda/evidence-standards-allegations/), [supervision with an exit](/reform/agenda/supervision-with-an-exit/), [objection treated as information](/reform/agenda/dissent-is-information/), and [outcome measurement](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/). Philosophy can help a father survive a broken process. It cannot fix the process. That takes rules.

## Conclusion

The Buddha compared his teaching to a raft. You build it to cross a river, and once you are across, you do not carry it on your back. The same goes for everything in this essay. If a line here helps you get through tonight, keep it. If it does not, put it down.
<!-- CITE: Alagaddupama Sutta (MN 22), simile of the raft. -->

What the emperor and the teacher agree on is small and hard. The first arrow is real, and you may not be able to stop it. The second arrow is in your own hand. Put it down as often as you can. Be your children's father fully in the time you are given, and survive the time you are not, so that you are still there, and still whole enough, when they come looking for you.
- **Be fully there in the time you have.** Do not spend supervised hours or alternate weekends interrogating the children about the other household, rehearsing the case, or grieving in front of them. Be their father for that time. That is the role done well. (On the structure of supervised time itself, see [Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/).)
