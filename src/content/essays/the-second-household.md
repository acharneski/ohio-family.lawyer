**Thesis.** In many separated families, grandparents become a second household. They provide daily care, transport, supervision of exchanges, and continuity. Ohio law treats that household as a resource while it exists and as a stranger once it is shut out. A system that relies on a caregiver's labor but gives the caregiver no standing has left part of the child's life off the ledger.

{% callout "not-claim" %}
This essay does not claim that grandparents should be able to override fit parents, that grandparents are always right about the family, or that any particular parent acted wrongly in limiting contact. Parents' decisions deserve real weight. The claim is about structure: who the system relies on, who it hears, and who it forgets.
{% endcallout %}

## I. The Second Household

Picture a common arrangement. A couple separates. One parent moves in with, or near, their own parents. Within months, the grandparents are doing much of the daily work: school runs at dawn, after-school snacks, therapy appointments, swim lessons, homework, bedtime on the nights the parent works or studies. They may also run every exchange with the other parent, because the parents can't be in the same room.

Over years, this is not babysitting. It is a household. The children have beds there, toys there, routines there. For a young child, it may be the most stable place in their life.

## II. Necessary, but Not a Party

The court case, meanwhile, is between two parents. The grandparents are not parties. They are not usually interviewed by the guardian ad litem unless someone insists. Their observations of how the children behave before and after parenting time, which parent attends to homework, how exchanges actually go, rarely reach the judge. In the terms of [Who Decides Who Counts](/essays/who-decides-who-counts/), they are not on the ledger.

They also occupy an odd position in the conflict. They may be told not to speak to the other parent at exchanges. They become informal supervisors and messengers without any status, any training, or any way to raise a concern except through the parent they live beside. When what they see doesn't match what is being said in court, they have no channel for it. Often, out of loyalty or fear of losing the grandchildren, they stay quiet.

## III. The Off Switch

Then something changes. There is a falling-out over money, a new partner, a move. The parent ends contact. Letters come back unopened. The children, who were there every day, are simply gone.

Under *Troxel v. Granville*, a fit parent's decision about who sees their child gets special weight, and for good reason. Parents, not relatives, are responsible for children. But Ohio gives grandparents a doorway into court only in specific circumstances: a divorce or related case, a parent's death, or a child born to unmarried parents. If the family doesn't fit those categories, or the case is closed, or the child has moved to another state, the grandparents may have no forum at all.
<!-- CITE: Troxel v. Granville, 530 U.S. 57 (2000); ORC 3109.051, 3109.11, 3109.12; ORC Chapter 3127 on interstate jurisdiction. -->

From the child's side, this is not a change in visitation. It is the loss of a household, often at the same time the child is losing contact with a parent. Nobody records it. No court made a finding that it was in the child's interest. It happened by default. In that respect it resembles supervised contact that ends when the payments stop ([Paying to Be Watched](/essays/paying-to-be-watched/)): a decision nobody had to make.

## IV. Witnesses Who Change Their Minds

There is a second, quieter cost. Grandparents who lived through the case often understood it one way at the time and another way later. Sometimes that's because they learned new facts. Sometimes it's because their own relationship with their adult child broke down, and the break reshaped their memory of everything before it.

Courts are right to be careful with that. A witness whose account shifts after a family rupture has an obvious motive. But the same caution means that the people who saw the most, for the longest, are often the least usable witnesses. A system that heard from them while they were still inside the household, routinely and neutrally, would have better evidence than one that hears from them only after they are angry. That is the principle argued in [Credibility Follows Evidence](/essays/credibility-follows-evidence/): weigh accounts by what supports them, and gather that support while it still exists.

## V. The Strongest Objections

{% callout "objections" %}
**Parents, not grandparents, decide who their children see.** *Troxel* exists for a reason, and relatives litigating against parents is a real intrusion.

**Grandparents are not neutral.** They are usually aligned with one parent, sometimes part of the conflict, and may enable one side's behavior.

**More parties means more litigation.** Adding relatives to custody cases could lengthen and intensify the fighting children are exposed to.
{% endcallout %}

**On parental authority.** Agreed. Nothing here would let relatives override a fit parent's ordinary decisions. The narrow proposal is that relatives who functioned as substantial caregivers, for a defined period, get a forum to be heard. The parent's decision still gets special weight there.

**On neutrality.** True, and that is an argument for hearing them early and in structured ways, through the GAL and through mediation, rather than only as partisan witnesses after a rupture. Bias is easier to weigh when you can see it.

**On conflict.** This is the serious one. Any expansion of standing should come with mediation first, screening for domestic violence, and limits on repetitive motions ([priority 6](/reform/agenda/weaken-conflict-gradient/)). The goal is continuity for the child, not another front in the parents' war.

{% callout "protections" %}
Where a parent limits relatives' contact because of abuse, threats, or danger, that decision stays protected, and emergency protection orders under ORC § 3113.31 remain fast and low-barrier. Nothing in this essay weakens a parent's ability to protect a child from a relative who poses a real risk.
{% endcallout %}

## VI. What Would Help

- **Caregiver standing.** Relatives who provided substantial, regular care for a defined period may ask a court to consider continued contact, whether or not a divorce case is open ([priority 11](/reform/agenda/extended-family-standing/)).
- **Hear caregivers while the case is live.** GALs should routinely interview adults who provide substantial care, and their reports should say whether they did. The best-interest factors in ORC § 3109.04(F)(1) already ask about the child's relationships with people who significantly affect the child's interest. The process should actually gather that evidence.
- **Count the transitions.** Outcome data should record when a child loses regular contact with a caregiver, not just a parent ([priority 4](/reform/agenda/outcome-measurement/)).
- **Mediation that includes the second household.** Where grandparents are doing the exchanges and the daily care, they belong at the table when schedules are set.

## Conclusion

Every separated family runs on someone's labor. Often a large share of it comes from grandparents who never filed anything, never testified, and never appear in the court file. When the arrangement holds, the system benefits from them. When it breaks, the system has no record that they existed.

A child's life is not divided neatly between two parents. An honest ledger would count everyone who was holding it together.
